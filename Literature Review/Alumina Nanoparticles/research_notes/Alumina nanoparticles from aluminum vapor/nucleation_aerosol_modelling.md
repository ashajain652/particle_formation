# Aerosol-dynamics modelling toolkit for alumina nanoparticles formed from cooling, reacting aluminum vapor (nucleation, condensation, coagulation, coalescence)

Scope note: these notes cover the modelling frameworks (nucleation theory, the aerosol general dynamic equation and its solution methods, coagulation kernels, coalescence/sintering laws, scaling results, atomistic studies and codes). Atmospheric chemistry of reentry, Al combustion kinetics and measured reentry particle sizes are covered by other researchers. Where a statement is my own derivation or estimate rather than a sourced fact, it is placed under "Inferences" and labelled as such. Several classic papers could not be opened; their content is described only as far as verified sources describe it, and they are listed at the end with citations.

## 1. Classical nucleation theory (CNT), its failure for refractory oxides, and the alternatives; property data for Al2O3

### Takeaway
For alumina formed from Al vapor plus oxygen, CNT is not a trustworthy predictor: the stable "monomer" Al2O3 is itself hard to form in the gas phase, small (Al2O3)n clusters have non-bulk ("magic-number") energetics, and at the very high supersaturations of oxide systems the critical nucleus is at or below one molecule, so particle formation is effectively collision-limited and the downstream size is set by coagulation, not by the nucleation rate. The modern alternatives are kinetic cluster models fed with quantum-chemical cluster free energies (Girshick-type kinetic nucleation; Gobrecht/Lam/Savel'ev-Starik-type chemical-kinetic cluster networks), with self-consistent CNT or Tolman-corrected CNT as a cheap fallback.

### Cited Findings
- Buesser & Pratsinis state that the fast chemistry typical of aerosol synthesis of oxides produces such high concentrations of product molecules that "their critical nucleation size at typical reactor conditions is smaller than that of a single monomer/molecule", so clusters grow immediately by coagulation; i.e. nucleation is collision-limited. They also note this macroscopic view "might not be the final answer" and cite West et al., whose 64-reaction TiCl4 oxidation mechanism gave a critical nucleus of five TiO2 molecules, and DFT work showing magic-number stability of (SiO2)N and (TiO2)N clusters. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012, Annu. Rev. Chem. Biomol. Eng. (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Because coagulation then dominates, Buesser & Pratsinis report that once particle formation ceases, a simple monodisperse model gives diameters "within 20% of those predicted by detailed models" of the full general dynamic equation; during the formation stage itself the mean diameter is badly underpredicted because the average mixes monomers with particles. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Gobrecht et al. (2022) computed (Al2O3)n clusters n = 1–10 with density-functional theory and built a chemical-kinetic network for alumina nucleation in AGB-star winds. They found that chemical-equilibrium calculations fail to predict alumina cluster formation; that successive addition of Al2O3 monomers is energetically viable; but that "the formation of the Al2O3 monomer itself represents an energetic bottleneck." They state that the applicability of CNT in such environments "has been questioned", and that the classical concept of surface tension is not applicable to sub-nanometre clusters. — [Gobrecht et al. 2022, A&A, arXiv:2110.11139](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11139)
- Gobrecht et al. fit the per-unit cluster energy as E(n) = a0 + a1 n^(−1/3) (excluding the monomer), with a0 = −1287.9 kJ/mol and a1 = 1075.42 kJ/mol (a1 is the term conventionally ascribed to surface tension); sizes n = 3, 4, 5 are more stable and n = 8, 10 less stable than this fit, i.e. magic-number effects. They used a "monomeric radius" of 2.08–2.60 Å. — [Gobrecht et al. 2022, arXiv:2110.11139](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11139)
- Gobrecht et al. caution that combustion conditions differ greatly from circumstellar envelopes, so their rates do not transfer directly. — [arXiv:2110.11139 (as summarised in search results)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11139)
- Lam et al. (Univ. Lyon) state that CNT fails for nanoparticle nucleation "because the atomistic nature of the seed has to be considered since geometrical structure as well as stoichiometry do not always match the bulk values." They computed stable AlxOy cluster structures and thermodynamics from first principles, computed the equilibrium composition of an Al–O gas versus temperature, pressure and Al:O ratio, validated against time-resolved spectroscopy of a laser-induced plasma from an Al2O3 target, and extrapolated to lower temperatures to propose the gas-composition pathway to the first alumina seeds. — Lam et al., "Atomistic Mechanisms for the Nucleation of Aluminium Oxide Nanoparticles" (PDF already in the folder as `Lam_2015_Atomistic_nucleation_alumina_nanoparticles.pdf`, saved by another researcher; I did not verify the journal metadata).
- Savel'ev & Starik (2018, Combustion and Flame) built a kinetic model of (Al2O3)n cluster formation and growth during combustion of aluminized fuels; for a 200 µm Al particle burning in O2/Ar they found rapid cluster growth in the first ~12 ms and argue cluster growth is the most likely route to alumina condensation nuclei. Thermodynamic data for n = 5–75 were extrapolated from quantum-chemical results for n = 2–4. — [Savel'ev & Starik 2018, Combust. Flame](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010218018302542)
- Girshick, Agarwal & Truhlar (2009) did a kinetic (cluster-by-cluster) nucleation calculation for pure Al vapor at 2000 K using quantum-mechanical free energies for Al clusters up to Al60. For saturation ratio S above about 4.5 the critical size locks at the magic number i = 55; steady-state rates are "many orders of magnitude lower" than CNT; onset of nucleation is predicted at S ≈ 13.3 versus ≈ 5.1 for CNT; above S ≈ 25 magic-numbered clusters are abundant enough to invalidate the monomer-addition-only assumption; transient times to steady state are much longer than CNT predicts. — [Girshick, Agarwal & Truhlar 2009, J. Chem. Phys. 131, 134305 (author copy, UMN)](https://comp.chem.umn.edu/Truhlar/docs/868.pdf)
- The same paper gives the self-consistent CNT (SCC, Girshick & Chiu 1990) free energy of forming an i-mer from monomers as ΔG_i/kT = θ(i^(2/3) − 1) − (i − 1) ln S, where θ is the dimensionless surface tension (θ = s1 σ/kT, s1 = monomer surface area). SCC sets the monomer's formation energy to zero; the standard (non-self-consistent) CNT rate is lower by a factor e^θ/S, which in their worked example was about 2000. In their transient test, SCC agreed "excellently" with the transient calculation when the capillarity free energies were used. — [Girshick et al. 2009](https://comp.chem.umn.edu/Truhlar/docs/868.pdf)
- Tacu, Khrabry & Kaganovich (2020) write the Girshick-corrected (self-consistent) nucleation rate as J = n1 n_sat v1 e^θ (2γ/(π m_a))^(1/2) exp(−4θ³/(27 (ln S)²)), with n1 the monomer number density, n_sat the saturation density, v1 the monomer volume, γ surface tension, m_a monomer mass. Their key observation: because J(t) is so steep in a cooling gas, "the expression of the nucleation rate, either from CNT or from kinetic nucleation theory, is of little importance in the determination of the nucleation burst time". — [Tacu et al. 2020, Phys. Rev. E 102, 022116 (PPPL copy)](https://w3.pppl.gov/~ikaganov/pdf_files/Tacu2020.pdf)
- Khrabry et al. (2024) computed Fe-cluster (2–100 atoms) free energies from MD and showed that the common spherical "capillarity" approximation with flat-surface tension distorts condensation dynamics and size distributions, whereas a size-dependent Tolman correction to surface tension reproduces the condensation timeline, mean size and distribution width of a cooling vapor (though not magic-number spikes); they give an updated analytical CNT rate including the size-dependent surface tension. — [Khrabry et al. 2024, arXiv:2408.16693](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16693)
- Liquid Al2O3 surface tension from aerodynamic-levitation measurements (Takatani, Muta, Fujieda et al.): σ = 0.632 − 2.310×10⁻⁵ (T − 2327) N/m, fitted over 2375–2770 K; same study fits density over 1978–2789 K and flags that trapped bubbles in earlier work can underestimate density. A separate levitation test gave about 0.72 N/m near 2500 K. (I saw only abstract-level summaries of these.) — [Int. J. Thermophys. record (x-mol)](https://ma.x-mol.com/paper/1737562338861223936); [DLR aerodynamic levitation record](https://elib.dlr.de/87127)
- Liquid Al2O3 density (older levitation work): ρ = (2.79 ± 0.01)[1 − (4.22 ± 0.14)×10⁻⁵ (T − 2500)] g/cm³ for 2000–3100 K; newer fits give about 2.92 g/cm³ near the melting point. — [J. Mater. Sci.-era levitation study (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1022650703233); newer value per the search summary of the Takatani/Muta/Fujieda study.
- The DLR levitation work measured molten alumina from its boiling point (3240 K) down to spontaneous crystallization at about 1860 K — i.e. levitated liquid alumina can supercool by roughly 470 K below the 2327 K melting point. — [DLR elib 87127](https://elib.dlr.de/87127)

### Inferences
- (My reasoning.) For Al vapor mixing with air, the condensable "Al2O3" does not exist as a vapor species in equilibrium; the gas holds Al, AlO, Al2O, AlO2, O, O2. "Supersaturation" must therefore be defined on a reaction basis (e.g. for 2Al + 3O → Al2O3(l), or AlO-based routes), which is the essence of "chemically driven" nucleation. With alumina's very low equilibrium vapor pressure, the effective supersaturation at 2000–3000 K is so large that the CNT critical nucleus is smaller than one formula unit, consistent with the collision-limited picture. The practical consequence: treat every new Al2O3 (or AlxOy) unit as a "particle" that grows by coagulation (Ulrich / Landgrebe-Pratsinis approach), or use a kinetic cluster network if early-stage composition matters.
- (My reasoning.) The Girshick-2009 Al result (critical size pinned at Al55, onset S ≈ 13 instead of 5) matters for the separate question of whether metallic Al droplets nucleate before oxidation in an O-poor core of a wake; it suggests CNT would predict Al nucleation too early.
- (My reasoning.) Input data needed for a CNT/SCC/Tolman calculation for alumina: σ ≈ 0.63–0.69 N/m near 2327–2500 K (temperature slope about −2.3×10⁻⁵ N/m/K), liquid density ≈ 2.8–2.9 g/cm³, molecular mass 102 g/mol, giving a molecular volume v1 = M/(ρ N_A) ≈ 102e-3/(2850 × 6.022e23) ≈ 5.9×10⁻²⁹ m³ (≈0.059 nm³) and a monomer radius ≈ 0.24 nm — consistent with Gobrecht's 2.08–2.60 Å. Solid α-alumina density (≈3.95–3.99 g/cm³) is higher; which value is used changes particle diameters by ~10% for a given mass.

## 2. Solving the aerosol general dynamic equation (GDE): monodisperse, moments, sectional, Monte Carlo; coagulation kernels; self-preserving distribution

### Takeaway
The GDE (coagulation + nucleation source + condensation/surface growth) can be solved at increasing cost by a monodisperse model, log-normal or quadrature moment methods (QMOM/TEMOM), sectional/nodal methods (1-D in volume, or 2-D in volume and surface area for aggregates), or Monte Carlo. Because concentrated oxide aerosols reach a self-preserving size distribution (geometric standard deviation about 1.45 for coalescing spheres) after growing only 2–3 times in size, a one- or two-moment model plus σg ≈ 1.45 is usually adequate once nucleation is over.

### Cited Findings
- The GDE for the particle-number density n(v,t) is: ∂n/∂t = ½∫ β(v−ṽ, ṽ) n(v−ṽ) n(ṽ) dṽ − n(v)∫ β(v, ṽ) n(ṽ) dṽ + S(v,t) δ(v − v_m) + ∂(I n)/∂v (coagulation gain and loss, nucleation at monomer volume v_m, condensation/surface growth). For fractal-like particles it is solved in two size dimensions (volume and surface area) by extending a 1-D sectional technique; moment methods close the moments by assuming a log-normal shape, interpolating between integer moments (MOMIC) or quadrature schemes, with the disadvantage that the distribution cannot be uniquely reconstructed. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Typical oxide synthesis aerosols have solid volume fractions of only 10⁻³–10⁻⁴; particles barely influence flow, so CFD fields can be computed first and particle dynamics solved along streamlines (one-way coupling). — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Highly concentrated aerosols reach a self-preserving size distribution (SPSD) "by the time they have grown to two to three times their initial size"; number-based σg = 1.44–1.46 thereafter; Granqvist & Buhrman's ten inert-gas-condensed metals all had σg = 1.48 ± 0.12. Coagulation can then be followed by dN/dt = −½βN² with d = (6V/πN)^(1/3). — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Agglomerates (non-coalescing) have collision diameter d_c = (6V/A)(A³/(36πV²))^(1/D_f) and fractal dimension D_f = 1.78 (diffusion-limited, continuum) or 1.89 (ballistic, free-molecular), prefactor k ≈ 1.4 ± 0.12 or 1.36 ± 0.10; their SPSD is slightly broader than for spheres. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Lehtinen & Zachariah (2001) showed the self-preserving logarithmic volume distribution exists for free-molecular, continuum and constant kernels, and gave fits φ_log/Φ_tot = ξ^a exp(−ξ^b) (ξ = v/v_mode) with a = 1.804, b = 0.9512 for the free-molecular kernel (a = 2.005, b = 1.002 continuum; a = 2, b = 1 constant); they solved the discrete coagulation equation with a geometric sectional grid v_k = b^k v_1 and a volume-conserving size-splitting operator. — [Lehtinen & Zachariah 2001, J. Colloid Interface Sci. 242:314 (author-group copy)](https://mrzgroup.engr.ucr.edu/media/431/download)
- Agglomerates formed by coagulation–agglomeration have a much broader SPSD than spheres (σg reported as 2.27 in the free-molecular regime versus ≈1.45 for spheres; this came from a search summary of Goudeli et al. and I did not verify which size measure the 2.27 refers to). — [Coagulation–agglomeration of fractal-like particles (CORE record)](https://core.ac.uk/works/49020266)
- Sectional coagulation-plus-sintering models: Park & Rogak (2003) 1-D sectional model with coagulation, sintering and surface growth (adding sintering increases primary size and slightly slows agglomerate growth); Schwade & Roth (2002) nucleation + coagulation with a shape variable (volume and surface area); Akhtar, Lipscomb & Pratsinis (1994) Monte Carlo coagulation and sintering. — [Park & Rogak 2003, AS&T](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/02786820300899); [Schwade & Roth 2002, J. Aerosol Sci.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021850202001866); [Akhtar et al. 1994, AS&T](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/02786829408959698)
- Tacu et al. compared their analytic moment results against the nodal GDE code NGDE (Prakash, Bapat & Zachariah 2003) with "excellent agreement" for cooling rates 10³–10⁶ K/s. — [Tacu et al. 2020](https://w3.pppl.gov/~ikaganov/pdf_files/Tacu2020.pdf)
- Mukherjee, Sonwane & Zachariah (2003) built a kinetic Monte Carlo model of collision plus coalescence that tracks particle temperature: the heat released when two nanoparticles coalesce (surface-energy release) can raise particles "many hundreds of degrees" above the gas, accelerating sintering; higher volume loading and lower pressure increase the effect, delaying aggregate formation and producing larger primaries (shown for Si and TiO2). — [Mukherjee et al. 2003, J. Chem. Phys. 119:3391 (author-group copy)](https://mrzgroup.ucr.edu/sites/default/files/2019-02/2003_jcp_KMC.pdf)
- Alumina-specific population-balance work: Sun, Zhang & Jiang (2026) coupled Euler–Lagrange simulation of Al combustion with a population balance solved by the Taylor-expansion method of moments (TEMOM) to predict alumina size distributions; TU Darmstadt's A-STEAM project is developing a quadrature-based moment method (QbMM) for alumina nanoparticles in the boundary layer of Al particles burning in steam; Poletaev & Khlebnikova (2019) modelled coagulation of monodisperse Al2O3 in a dust flame including Coulomb repulsion and ion-wind effects. — [Sun et al. 2026 (Francis Press)](https://francis-press.com/papers/20714); [TU Darmstadt A-STEAM](https://www.maschinenbau.tu-darmstadt.de/asteam/research_a_steam/nanoparticles/index.en.jsp); [Poletaev & Khlebnikova 2019, J. Chem. (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/9799f3d5a25d4407b7abfab922250254)

### Inferences
- (Standard textbook relations, Friedlander 2000 / Seinfeld & Pandis; not re-verified online in this session.) Free-molecular kernel for spheres: β(v_i,v_j) = (3/4π)^(1/6) (6kT/ρ_p)^(1/2) (1/v_i + 1/v_j)^(1/2) (v_i^(1/3) + v_j^(1/3))²; continuum: β = (2kT/3μ)(1/v_i^(1/3) + 1/v_j^(1/3))(v_i^(1/3) + v_j^(1/3)) with a Cunningham slip correction; Fuchs' interpolation bridges the two regimes using the particle Knudsen number. A finite SPSD correction multiplies the monodisperse free-molecular kernel by about 6.67/5.66 ≈ 1.18 (Lee/Vemury-Pratsinis value; recalled, not verified).
- (My derivation from dN/dt = −½βN² with the free-molecular equal-sphere kernel β = 4√2 (3/4π)^(1/6)(6kT/ρ)^(1/2) v^(1/6) and constant solid volume fraction φ.) Mean particle volume grows as v ∝ (φ t)^(6/5) T^(3/5), so diameter d ∝ φ^(2/5) t^(2/5) T^(1/5) — the classic "Ulrich" scaling. In continuum regime (kernel independent of size for equal spheres) N decays as 1/t and d ∝ (φ t kT/μ)^(1/3).
- (My estimate, illustrative.) At 2000 K for 2 nm alumina (ρ = 3000 kg/m³) the equal-sphere free-molecular kernel is about 1.3×10⁻¹⁵ m³/s (1.3×10⁻⁹ cm³/s), so the characteristic coagulation time τ_c = 2/(βN) is ≈15 ms at N = 10¹⁷ m⁻³ and ≈1.5 s at 10¹⁵ m⁻³. In a reentry wake the vapour is diluted in a rapidly expanding, low-density flow, so N falls with time and coagulation can "freeze" early; this is why mixing/dilution history matters as much as cooling rate.
- (My reasoning.) At mesospheric pressures the gas mean free path is of order 0.1–1 mm, so every nanoparticle is deep in the free-molecular regime; continuum kernels are irrelevant except possibly in the dense shock layer.

## 3. Coalescence/sintering of alumina and the coagulation–coalescence competition

### Takeaway
Primary-particle size is set where the characteristic coalescence (sintering) time τ_s, which grows steeply as temperature falls and as particles grow (≈ d for viscous flow, ≈ d⁴ for grain-boundary diffusion), becomes comparable to the characteristic collision time τ_c; above that point colliding particles fuse into spheres, below it they form hard aggregates and finally soft agglomerates. For liquid alumina above its 2327 K melting point (and in supercooled liquid down to perhaps ~1860–2000 K) viscous-flow coalescence takes nanoseconds, so particles stay spherical; the primary size is therefore fixed during cooling through the freezing/solid-state-diffusion window. An alumina-specific solid-state sintering-time correlation exists (Johannessen et al. 2000 fit two coalescence parameters to flame-made alumina), but I could not open it to quote the numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Surface-area relaxation law (Koch & Friedlander 1990): da/dt = −(a − a_fc)/τ_s, where a is aggregate surface area and a_fc that of the fully coalesced sphere; τ_s is the time to remove 63% of excess area and depends on material, size and temperature. It has quantitatively explained coalescence of SiO2, TiO2, B4C, Ag and PdO when τ_s parameters are adjusted. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Three-stage picture: early, τ_c ≫ τ_s and particles fully coalesce; at τ_c ≈ τ_s sinter necks form (onset of aggregation); later, as T drops, τ_s → ∞, primary size freezes and only soft agglomerates form (τ_c ≪ τ_s). The duration of the τ_c ≈ τ_s stage controls whether product is agglomerates of aggregates or of spheres. Sintering narrows the primary-particle size distribution because sintering rates are inversely proportional to diameter. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- MD shows TiO2 sintering shifts from surface to grain-boundary diffusion and reaches bulk sintering rates at about 5 nm diameter, i.e. bulk τ_s laws fail for the smallest particles. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Johannessen, Pratsinis & Livbjerg (2000) modelled flame synthesis of alumina from Al-tri-sec-butoxide with coagulation + coalescence along CFD trajectories and estimated two coalescence-kinetic parameters by fitting measured specific surface area; this fitted alumina coalescence law "can be used to predict the surface area and shape of the particles". A later paper notes that alumina, having the lowest diffusion coefficient of the oxides compared, reached geometric volume-mean diameters of 2.8–10.7 nm. — [Johannessen et al. 2000, Chem. Eng. Sci. 55:177 (DTU Orbit record)](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/computational-fluid-particle-dynamics-for-the-flame-synthesis-of-/); [Carbothermal-reduction paper citing it (AS&T)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/02786820290110978)
- A related same-group paper treats coalescence as surface diffusion with a curvature-dependent energy barrier (surface melting), which speeds coalescence of touching nanoparticles and gives absolute sizes in fair agreement with experiments for vapor-derived alumina and titania. — [J. Nanopart. Res. (Springer), doi:10.1023/A:1010021004233](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1010021004233)
- Chakraborty & Zachariah (2014) used ReaxFF reactive MD on oxide-coated Al nanoparticles: core Al cations diffuse into the alumina shell, forming a sub-oxide that melts below alumina's melting point; two 8 nm coated particles heated to 2000 K (≈400 K below alumina's melting point) fused completely in 0.7 ns. They used the Frenkel viscous-flow law for liquid-state fusion (τ ∝ η d/σ, η viscosity, σ surface tension), scaling to ≈5 ns for 50 nm particles, and Hawa & Zachariah's aggregate correction τ ∝ (N − 1)^(0.68 D_f) for N primaries. — [Chakraborty & Zachariah 2014, Combust. Flame 161:1408 (author copy)](https://mrzgroup.engr.ucr.edu/media/1446/download)
- Mukherjee et al. (2003): exothermic coalescence heating delays aggregation and increases primary size — relevant because Al oxidation and alumina coalescence are both strongly exothermic. — [Mukherjee et al. 2003](https://mrzgroup.ucr.edu/sites/default/files/2019-02/2003_jcp_KMC.pdf)
- Gramatte et al. (2022, COMB3 MD) found that 6 and 10 nm γ-Al2O3 nanoparticles undergo a crystalline-to-amorphous transformation at 900 K starting at the reconstructed surface, with an Al-enriched surface and an Al-depleted transition layer — i.e. alumina nanoparticles may be amorphous and compositionally heterogeneous, which alters diffusion/sintering relative to bulk corundum. — [Gramatte et al. 2022, arXiv:2211.16081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.16081)

### Inferences
- (My estimate.) Viscous (Frenkel-type) coalescence of molten alumina: τ_s ≈ η d/σ. With σ ≈ 0.63 N/m and a melt viscosity of order 0.03–0.1 Pa·s (unverified; see Gaps), a 10 nm droplet coalesces in ≈0.5–1.6 ns and a 100 nm droplet in ≈5–16 ns — many orders of magnitude faster than any coagulation time in a reentry wake (milliseconds to seconds). So above the melting/supercooling limit, alumina particles are compact spheres and size grows purely by coagulation; aggregation can only begin once particles solidify.
- (My reasoning.) Because levitated alumina supercools to ≈1860 K and nanoparticles have depressed melting points, the "freeze-out" temperature for coalescence of nanometre alumina is uncertain by several hundred kelvin; the predicted primary size is sensitive to this choice. A sensitivity study bracketing the coalescence cut-off between ≈1800 and 2327 K is advisable.
- (My reasoning, from the τ_c ≈ τ_s criterion; Lehtinen, Windeler & Friedlander 1996.) Primary size d_p* solves τ_s(d_p*, T) = τ_c(N, d_p*, T). Since τ_s rises steeply with d (d¹ viscous, d⁴ grain-boundary) and exponentially as T falls, faster cooling freezes d_p at smaller values, and lower concentration (larger τ_c) lets particles coalesce to larger sizes at a given temperature but yields fewer collisions overall.

## 4. How cooling rate and initial vapor concentration control final size; quantitative scaling laws

### Takeaway
Two stages set the size. (i) In the nucleation burst, Tacu, Khrabry & Kaganovich derive that the post-burst mean diameter is proportional to the initial monomer concentration and inversely proportional to the cooling rate (valid for cooling rates up to ~10⁶ K/s). (ii) Afterwards, coagulation dominates and erases the memory of nucleation: in the free-molecular regime d grows as (φ t)^(2/5) (φ = condensed volume fraction, t = time at high temperature), while coalescence-freeze-out determines the primary size. Faster cooling and stronger dilution therefore give smaller, more numerous particles.

### Cited Findings
- Tacu et al. (2020): using Friedlander's moment model, the nucleation-burst time t0 satisfies t0/τ_cooling = θ0/(27 W(τ_cooling/τ_collision)) with W the Lambert function, τ_cooling = e_s T0/(e_a Ṫ0) (e_a activation energy of the saturation-pressure law, Ṫ0 cooling rate) and τ_collision = 4/(v0 V1^(2/3) n0) (monomer collision time). Conclusion: "the mean diameter is proportional to the initial monomer concentration and inversely proportional to the cooling rate", with nucleation and coagulation less well separated at high cooling rates; verified against the NGDE nodal code for 10³–10⁶ K/s. For Al vapor at T0 = 1773 K, monomer consumption by nucleation is negligible relative to deposition (ratio ≈10 at 10⁶ K/s and ≈1000 at 10³ K/s). — [Tacu et al. 2020](https://w3.pppl.gov/~ikaganov/pdf_files/Tacu2020.pdf)
- Khrabry et al. (2024) restate this as: nanoparticle diameter roughly proportional to vapor density and inversely proportional to cooling rate. — [Khrabry et al. 2024, arXiv:2408.16693](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16693)
- A thermal-plasma Si nanoparticle study reports that higher quench rates give higher number density, smaller size and smaller standard deviation. — [Nanomaterials 2021 (PMC8224306)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8224306/)
- Panda & Pratsinis (1995) modelled Al nanoparticle formation by evaporation–condensation in an aerosol flow reactor: argon saturated with Al vapor cooled from 1600 °C at about 1000 °C/s in an 18 mm-diameter, 0.5 m-long tube, with nucleation and growth along the tube (description via a University of Michigan reactor-engineering module; I could not open the paper or the full module). — [U. Michigan Elements aerosol module (search snippet)](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~elements/5e/web_mod/aerosol/frame01_introd/introduction.htm)
- A 2008 nodal-GDE simulation of Al nanoparticle synthesis by evaporation–condensation found low pressure and low vaporization temperature favour smaller, more uniform particles, and that cooling rate had only a modest effect over the range studied. — [ITS record, Chem. Prod. Process Model. 2008](https://scholar.its.ac.id/en/publications/modeling-and-simulation-of-aluminum-nanoparticle-synthesis-by-the/)
- Typical flame/reactor sequence: precursor consumed early, then coagulation + sintering as T falls; in the illustrative case of Buesser & Pratsinis, τ_c ≈ τ_s at t ≈ 8×10⁻⁵ s and sintering stops by t ≈ 4×10⁻³ s. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- Flame-made alumina with fitted coalescence kinetics: geometric volume-mean diameters 2.8–10.7 nm. — [AS&T paper citing Johannessen et al.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/02786820290110978)

### Inferences
- (My synthesis.) Tacu's "d ∝ n0/Ṫ" applies only to the nucleation-burst stage; for refractory oxides with collision-limited nucleation the burst stage is essentially instantaneous and the final size is set by coagulation + coalescence freeze-out, i.e. by d ∝ (φ τ_hot)^(2/5) T^(1/5) up to the freeze-out time τ_hot. Both point the same way: dilution (lower φ) and fast cooling (short τ_hot) give smaller particles, but the exponents differ (linear vs 2/5), so the model must resolve which stage dominates.
- (My reasoning.) For reentry, the relevant "cooling rate" is set by mixing of hot ablation vapour into cold ambient air and by wake expansion, which simultaneously dilutes φ; both effects shrink final size, so very small (few-nm) primary particles are plausible at high altitude, with larger particles where molten droplets are shed directly (a separate pathway handled elsewhere).

### Gaps
- Ulrich (1971), Panda & Pratsinis (1995), Okuyama et al. and Kruis et al. could not be opened; their specific numbers (e.g. Panda & Pratsinis predicted Al sizes) are not reported here.
- No study found that applies these scalings to alumina at reentry (low pressure, strongly diluting, oxidizing) conditions.

## 5. Molecular-dynamics, ReaxFF and machine-learning-potential studies of Al–O cluster nucleation and growth

### Takeaway
Atomistic work on alumina formation from vapor is dominated by first-principles cluster thermochemistry (Gobrecht 2022; Lam et al.; Savel'ev & Starik) and ReaxFF reactive MD of Al oxidation (Hong & van Duin 2015; Chakraborty & Zachariah 2014; Ferreira et al. 2024 for satellite reentry). I found no machine-learning potential trained specifically for gas-phase Al–O cluster nucleation; recent ML potentials address Al solidification and Al-nanoparticle oxidation.

### Cited Findings
- Ferreira et al. (2024, GRL) report the first atomic-scale (ReaxFF) MD study of satellite aluminum oxidation during mesospheric reentry: a typical 250 kg satellite yields about 30 kg of aluminum oxide nanoparticles that may persist for decades; mega-constellation reentries could produce over 360 t/yr of aluminum oxides. (PDF already saved in the folder by another researcher.) — [Ferreira et al. 2024, GRL, doi:10.1029/2024GL109280 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/aaecbc3158d24d57ad117fc9a58fba9c); [AMOS 2023 poster](https://amostech.com/TechnicalPapers/2023/Poster/Ferreira.pdf)
- Hong & van Duin (2015) ReaxFF MD of Al nanoparticle oxidation at 300, 500 and 900 K: O adsorption/dissociation hot spots create voids that lower the O-diffusion barrier; agreement with experimental oxidation kinetics. — [LAMMPS abstract](https://lammps.org/abstracts/abstract.6354.html)
- Chakraborty & Zachariah (2014) ReaxFF: Al-cation diffusion softens the alumina shell, allowing sintering well below alumina's melting point (details in §3). — [Combust. Flame 2014 (author copy)](https://mrzgroup.engr.ucr.edu/media/1446/download)
- Gobrecht et al. (2022) DFT global-minimum searches for (Al2O3)n, n ≤ 10, with new global-minimum candidates for n = 7, 9, 10. — [arXiv:2110.11139](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11139)
- Machine-learning potentials: Lu et al. (2025 preprint) trained an ML potential (energy RMSE 1.2 meV/atom, force RMSE 0.126 eV/Å) scalable to million-atom Al-nanoparticle oxidation and report a temperature-regulated dual-mode oxidation mechanism with the oxide shell acting as a "gatekeeper"; other ML potentials (Jakse et al. 2023; Tipeev & Zanotto) address crystallization of pure Al. — [Lu et al. 2025, arXiv:2512.22529](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22529); [Jakse et al., arXiv:2201.01370](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01370)
- Khrabry et al. (2024) show how MD-derived cluster free energies can be passed into a kinetic condensation model (Fe), a template for doing the same with Al–O potentials. — [arXiv:2408.16693](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16693)

### Inferences
- (My reasoning.) The most direct route to alumina-specific nucleation inputs is: DFT/ML cluster free energies (Gobrecht, Lam) for AlxOy clusters, fitted with an n^(−1/3) (Tolman-like) law beyond n ≈ 10, fed into a kinetic cluster model (Girshick/Khrabry style) that hands off to a moment or sectional coagulation model at a few nm.

### Gaps
- No ML potential for gas-phase Al–O clustering found; ReaxFF Al/O parameter sets are known to have accuracy limits at high temperature (not verified here).
- Ferreira et al.'s particle-size predictions were not checked by me against aerosol-dynamics theory; their MD timescales (ns) are far shorter than coagulation timescales (ms–s).

## 6. Practical codes and approaches a modeller could adopt

### Takeaway
A tractable pipeline for reentry alumina: gas-phase Al/O chemistry (e.g. Cantera with an Al–O mechanism) along a wake trajectory, coupled to (a) collision-limited "nucleation" of Al2O3 units or a small kinetic cluster network, (b) a monodisperse or log-normal moment model with free-molecular kernel and the Koch–Friedlander surface-area relaxation law (two-moment + area), checked against (c) a sectional/nodal code (NGDE-type) for the size distribution; the resulting size/number then initializes an atmospheric sectional model (CARMA in WACCM) or modal model (MAM in CAM/CESM) for long-term evolution.

### Cited Findings
- WACCM coupled with the sectional aerosol model CARMA has been used to simulate 10 Gg/yr of reentry alumina: a 20–40 Gg stratospheric burden accumulates poleward of 30° between 10 and 30 km within 1–2 years, depending on reentry latitude and particle size; longwave cooling near the stratopause gave temperature anomalies up to 1.5 K (Maloney et al., EGU 2025). — [EGU25-3866](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-3866.html); [EGU26-19003 (initial-radius sensitivity)](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-19003.html)
- An ESSOAR preprint estimated alumina lifetimes of 1–2 years and radiative forcing of about −0.378 mW/m², with fine particles giving larger forcing and longer lifetimes than coarse ones. — [ESSOAR preprint](https://essopenarchive.org/doi/full/10.22541/essoar.170110669.90121203)
- A CESM user forum thread documents adding an alumina aerosol emission to CAM6 via the 4-mode Modal Aerosol Module (MAM4). — [CESM forum](https://bb.cgd.ucar.edu/cesm/threads/add-alumina-aerosol-emission-to-cam6-via-mam-4.7711)
- Moment methods have been implemented in CFD for flame reactors (log-normal, MOMIC, quadrature), including RANS, LES and DNS couplings; turbulent diffusion flames making Al2O3 and TiO2 were simulated with the Reynolds-stress model to extract effective sintering rates. — [Buesser & Pratsinis 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3591782/)
- NGDE nodal GDE solver used as a benchmark for metal-vapor condensation. — [Tacu et al. 2020](https://w3.pppl.gov/~ikaganov/pdf_files/Tacu2020.pdf)
- Omnisoot: an open object-oriented process-design package for gas-phase synthesis of carbonaceous nanoparticles (sectional/moment particle models coupled to gas chemistry), a possible template. — [arXiv:2507.10879](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10879)
- TEMOM (Euler–Lagrange + PBE for alumina in Al combustion) and QbMM (TU Darmstadt) are alumina-specific moment implementations. — [Sun et al. 2026](https://francis-press.com/papers/20714); [TU Darmstadt](https://www.maschinenbau.tu-darmstadt.de/asteam/research_a_steam/nanoparticles/index.en.jsp)

### Inferences
- (My recommendation.) Start with a 0-D Lagrangian parcel model (temperature and dilution history from the wake model) carrying: Al-species chemistry; collision-limited formation of Al2O3; a two-moment (N, V) plus surface-area (A) particle model with the free-molecular kernel, SPSD σg ≈ 1.45 for reconstruction, and da/dt = −(a − a_fc)/τ_s with τ_s = Frenkel viscous law above a freeze temperature and a solid-state law below. Validate the size predictions against a sectional run, then bracket the uncertain inputs (freeze-out temperature, sintering law, dilution rate). This captures the leading-order dependence on cooling rate and concentration at very low cost.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm a public Cantera Al–O mechanism or a published Cantera–sectional coupling for alumina.
