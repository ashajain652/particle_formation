# Alumina nanoparticle formation from aluminum vapor: analogue systems (laser ablation, thermal plasma, wire explosion, meteor ablation, solid rocket motors) and the spacecraft-reentry literature

Scope note. These notes cover the analogue systems and the reentry-specific literature only; aluminum particle combustion and nucleation theory are covered by other researchers. Papers marked "(downloaded)" are saved as PDFs in the Alumina Nanoparticles folder and were read in full text with PyMuPDF; everything else rests on abstracts, conference listings or search-result summaries and is marked as such. Numbers are quoted as the sources give them. Where I computed something myself, it is in the "Inferences" subsections and labelled as my calculation.

## 1. Pulsed laser ablation of aluminum in O2, air, Ar–O2 and liquids: plume chemistry, AlO emission as an oxidation tracer, and particle size versus background pressure

### Takeaway
Laser ablation of aluminum in oxygen-bearing gas produces alumina primary particles of only a few nanometres (radius about 3 to 7 nm), which then stick together into fractal agglomerates tens of nanometres across. More oxygen, higher ambient pressure and higher laser fluence all give larger particles. AlO emission shows the gas-phase oxidation step directly: AlO forms tens of microseconds after the pulse, as the plume cools from about 6000 K to about 3000 K, and Al2O3-stoichiometry clusters appear later still, at 100 to 500 microseconds.

### Cited Findings
- **Kautz et al. (2023), J. Chem. Phys. (downloaded).** Nanosecond laser ablation (1064 nm, 6 ns pulses) of AA6061 aluminum alloy at about 750 Torr, comparing flowing dry air (about 20 % O2) with N2 (99.99 %) and with 5 % and 20 % O2 in N2. With about 20 % O2 the plume shows strong AlO emission. In N2 with trace O2 it shows AlN and strong unreacted Al emission instead. — [Kautz et al. 2023, accepted manuscript via OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2204679); DOI [10.1063/5.0167400](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0167400)
- Kautz et al. measured plasma temperature in stages. From Al II lines, the excitation temperature falls from about 16,000 K to about 12,000 K between about 0.5 and 5 µs in both air and N2. From the AlO (0,1) band head near 508 nm, the AlO molecular temperature in air falls from about 6000 K to about 3000 K between about 5 and 60 µs. At 750 Torr the early-time plasma conditions are not significantly changed by the presence of O2. — [Kautz et al. 2023](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2204679)
- In air, AlO formation is prominent at 10 to 100 µs, with no strong AlN signal. In-situ mass spectrometry finds Al oxide cluster ions with roughly Al2O3 stoichiometry (Al2xO3x+, with an O:Al ratio of about 1.5) at 100 to 500 µs. These clusters grow larger as the O2 fraction rises from 5 % to 20 %. — [Kautz et al. 2023](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2204679)
- Agglomerates made in air are larger than those made in N2. High-resolution TEM finds Al2O3 nanoparticles even in nominal N2 with only trace O2, alongside AlN nanoparticles. — [Kautz et al. 2023](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2204679)
- Kautz et al. sized the agglomerates by vacuum aerodynamic diameter (from a single-particle mass spectrometer) and by mobility diameter. The vacuum aerodynamic diameter distributions are bimodal, with a narrow first peak at about 50 nm and a broad peak at larger sizes. The mobility diameter of the agglomerates increases with oxygen content. The fractal dimension is about 2, and the effective density is far below the bulk Al2O3 density of 3.95 g cm−3, which points to small primary particles inside open, fractal agglomerates. — [Kautz et al. 2023](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2204679)
- The same group's earlier work ablated a multi-component target (10 % Al, 30 % Ti, 20 % Zr, 20 % Nb, 15 % Ta, 5 % V) at high cooling rates in atmospheric-pressure air. It produced amorphous nanoparticles under 10 nm in diameter that formed fractal agglomerates. Kautz et al. list the main Al–O formation channel as Al + O2 ⇌ AlO + O, followed by higher oxides, clusters and nucleation. They also note that metal-oxide formation in laser plasmas typically occurs below 8000 K. — [Kautz et al. 2023](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2204679)
- **Johnston et al. (1992), J. Am. Ceram. Soc. (abstract only).** Reactive laser ablation of an aluminum target in O2 gave nanosize alumina with surface areas of 135 to 250 m²/g and primary particle radii of 3 to 7 nm. Particle size increased, and surface area decreased, with both increasing O2 pressure and increasing laser fluence. On heating, the powders went directly from γ-alumina to α-alumina at about 1200 °C, skipping the other transition phases. The numeric O2 pressure range is not given in the abstract. — [Johnston et al. 1992, Scholars' Mine record](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/matsci_eng_facwork/224); DOI [10.1111/j.1151-2916.1992.tb04424.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1151-2916.1992.tb04424.x)
- **Stuttgart plasma wind tunnel (EGU 2026 abstract).** Aluminum powder embedded in epoxy and ablated in an arc-heated plasma flow evaporated and formed AlO, identified from known AlO bands. Powder injected against the flow through a water-cooled probe also produced AlO. Solid aluminum alone shed large molten droplets instead. — [Kuenstler et al., EGU26-1943](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-1943.html)
- **Leeds kinetics work.** The Leeds group used pulsed laser ablation of an Al target in a fast flow tube as an Al and AlO source for kinetics. It measured k(AlO+ + CO) = (3.7 ± 1.1) × 10−10 and k(AlO+ + O) = (1.7 ± 0.7) × 10−10 cm³ molecule−1 s−1 at 294 K. — [Plane et al. 2021, JGR Space Physics (downloaded)](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JA028792)

### Inferences
- My calculation from Kautz et al.'s AlO temperatures: a drop of about 3000 K over about 55 µs is a cooling rate of roughly 5 × 10^7 K/s at atmospheric pressure. This is far faster than the cooling of a reentry wake at 60 to 90 km. AlO appears only once the plume has cooled to about 6000 K or below, and Al2O3 clusters only after about 100 µs. Gas-phase AlO and AlO2 formation therefore comes before nucleation, rather than nucleation acting directly on Al atoms. That is consistent with the AlO-then-OAlO-then-clusters sequence used in combustion models.
- Primary particle size appears fairly insensitive to conditions: it is a few nanometres in radius in both Johnston et al. and Kautz et al. What changes with oxygen availability and pressure is mainly the agglomerate size. For a reentry model, this suggests treating primary-particle formation (nucleation plus coalescence) separately from agglomeration (coagulation of solid primaries).
- Pressure trend: higher ambient pressure confines the plume, keeps vapor dense and gives larger particles. At 60 to 90 km, where the ambient pressure is about 20 Pa down to about 0.2 Pa (US Standard Atmosphere 1976 values, not from the sources above), plumes expand far more freely. If the trend holds, reentry primaries should sit at or below the small end of the laboratory range, unless the vapor is held in a dense wake.

### Gaps
- I found no study of aluminum ablation in O2 at low background pressure (around 1 to 100 Pa) that measured AlO emission and particle size together. Kautz et al. worked only at 750 Torr, and Johnston et al.'s abstract does not give pressures.
- Laser ablation of Al in liquids (water, ethanol), which reportedly yields alumina or boehmite nanoparticles of roughly 10 to 50 nm, was not verified. The candidate open-access sources were not fetched successfully: a Hindawi J. Nanomaterials 2012 paper (doi 10.1155/2012/819403) returned HTTP 403, and an MDPI 2020 laser-ablation-in-liquids review was not read. Liquid ablation is also a poor analogue for a rarefied gas.

## 2. Thermal (RF/DC arc) plasma synthesis and electrical explosion of Al wires: sizes, phases and the role of quench rate

### Takeaway
Thermal plasma synthesis of alumina, whether from Al powder burned in an oxygen plasma or from Al2O3 feed that is evaporated and then quenched, gives mean particle sizes of about 15 to 50 nm. The crystal phases are metastable transition aluminas, δ and γ. Stronger quenching favours δ over α, and a higher precursor feed rate (more vapor) gives larger, more agglomerated particles. Electrical explosion of Al wires gives nanoparticles of a few to about 65 nm, with size increasing with ambient gas pressure and wire diameter. Oxide formed in oxygen-bearing gas is reported as γ-Al2O3. Quantitative relations between size and quench rate were not found in open sources.

### Cited Findings
- **DC thermal plasma, Al powder in an oxygen plasma (Universitas Jember).** The metal evaporates and oxidizes to alumina. Mean sizes were 36.55 nm at a powder feed of 1.16 g/min and 46.49 nm at 3.19 and 3.5 g/min. A higher feed rate increased agglomeration. The products were dominantly γ- and δ-alumina, with phase intensities of at least 75 %. One summary reports all samples as δ-Al2O3. Source: Indonesian-language journal and repository abstract; the full text was not read. — [Jember repository record](https://repository.unej.ac.id/handle/123456789/105529); [RTR journal article](https://jseahr.jurnal.unej.ac.id/index.php/RTR/article/view/5142)
- **High-pressure RF thermal plasma (ORNL, IEEE Trans. Plasma Sci. 2014).** The feed was alumina chunks, not Al metal. The product had a peak size of 15 nm, a narrow distribution (half-width about 24 nm), no agglomeration, good crystallinity, and mixed α- and δ-alumina. — [ORNL publication record](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/characteristics-of-synthesized-alumina-nanoparticles-in-a-high-pr/); DOI [10.1109/TPS.2014.2299871](https://doi.org/10.1109/tps.2014.2299871)
- **Inductively coupled RF plasma (EPFL / Empa thesis 2006 and AIChE 2006).** XRD shows a phase change from α- to δ-alumina at low central gas flow, which the authors associate with higher quenching efficiency. High central gas flow gives θ-alumina. Lower central gas flow also gave higher plasma enthalpy, laminar powder flow, more precursor evaporation and more nanoparticles. Emission spectroscopy of Al(g) and AlO(g) was used to find the best axial position for injecting the quench gas, which is the same AlO-as-oxidation-tracer approach as in laser ablation. — [EPFL Infoscience record 85792](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/85792?ln=en); [AIChE 2006 proceeding](https://proceedings.aiche.org/conferences/aiche-spring-meeting-and-global-congress-on-process-safety/2006/proceeding/paper/12c-situ-process-monitoring-plasma-synthesis-alumina-nanoparticles); thesis DOI [10.5075/epfl-thesis-3564](https://doi.org/10.5075/epfl-thesis-3564)
- **Wire explosion in argon with oxygen or air.** Nazarenko, Ilyin & Tikhonov (Adv. Mater. Res. 872, 2013) exploded Al, Ti and Cu wires in argon mixed with 0.5 to 30 vol % O2 or air. Only the abstract was found, and it gives no sizes or phases. — [TPU library record](https://vufind.lib.tpu.ru/vufind/Record/646770)
- **Wire explosion, IIT Madras.** The nanopowder formed was crystalline γ-Al2O3. The authors note that the local temperature of the medium during formation decides the phase. — [IIT Madras publication record](https://publications.iitm.ac.in/publication/studies-on-generation-and-characterization-of-nanoalumina-powder)
- **Wire explosion, High Power Laser and Particle Beams (Chinese journal).** The average size of Al2O3 nanoparticles made by wire explosion was 64.9 nm, increasing with gas pressure and wire diameter. Source: search summary only. — [HPLPB article](https://www.hplpb.com.cn/article/id/2414)
- **Wire explosion in argon (no oxygen).** Aluminum nanopowder morphology depends mainly on argon pressure rather than deposited energy. The count mean diameter falls monotonically as the plasma expansion parameter rises, and higher deposited energy narrows the distribution. Source: search summaries of 2014 and 2016 studies. — [Scientific.net KEM 712](https://www.scientific.net/KEM.712.20); [HPLPB 2016](https://www.hplpb.com.cn/en/article/doi/10.11884/HPLPB201628.160068)
- **Pervikov et al. (2021), Materials.** Exploding Al wires in argon at 0.2 MPa gives nanoparticles under 100 nm together with a submicron fraction above 100 nm. Nanopowder forms only when the energy input exceeds the metal's sublimation energy (33 J/mm³ for Al). At fixed specific energy and gas pressure, mean nanoparticle size grows with wire diameter up to about 0.3 mm. — [Pervikov et al. 2021, PMC8585286](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8585286); DOI 10.3390/ma14216602
- **Kwon et al. (2007, arXiv).** Al and Cu wire-explosion nanoparticles with a characteristic size of about 3 to 40 nm oxidize and crystallize when passivated in air. Al nanopowders made in argon contained about 88 to 91 wt % metallic Al after passivation, with an oxidation onset of 530 to 550 °C. — [Kwon et al., arXiv:0708.1828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0708.1828)

### Inferences
- Across thermal plasma and wire explosion, the controls on size are the same as in laser ablation: vapor concentration (feed rate, wire energy, wire diameter) and ambient gas density or pressure. Higher vapor concentration and higher pressure give larger particles and more agglomeration, and faster quenching gives smaller particles. This is the standard picture in which coagulation growth scales with vapor number density multiplied by residence time before the particles freeze. A reentry model should therefore treat the vapor dilution history of the wake (entrainment rate) as at least as important as its temperature history.
- On phase, rapid quench of alumina vapor or droplets gives metastable γ or δ alumina, not α, in every analogue found here. The exception is the ORNL case, which mixed in α. Alumina nanoparticles formed in a reentry wake are therefore likely to be transition aluminas or amorphous. This matters for refractive index and surface chemistry (chlorine activation), but it is my extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No open source gave a quantitative relation between particle size and quench rate in K/s for alumina plasma synthesis. Typical thermal plasma quench rates of about 10^5 to 10^7 K/s are widely quoted, but I did not verify them in a source during this search.
- No open-access full text for wire explosion in O2 or air with measured sizes and phases was obtained.

## 3. Meteor ablation and meteoric smoke particles as a direct analogue (70–110 km)

### Takeaway
Meteoric metals ablate at about 80 to 110 km. They are oxidized by O3, O2 and H2O to stable reservoir molecules (FeOH, Mg(OH)2, SiO2, and AlOH for aluminum), which polymerize without a free-energy barrier into "meteoric smoke particles" (MSPs). Models start the smoke at a radius of about 0.2 nm (molecular size). Radar observations give MSP radii of about 0.6 to 1.5 nm at 80 to 95 km. Laboratory analogues give primary particles of about 6 nm radius that form fractal aggregates. Global models use radii of 1 to 100 nm. Growth is set by Brownian coagulation and is highly uncertain. For aluminum specifically, the Leeds chemistry finds AlOH, not alumina, to be the main gas-phase reservoir.

### Cited Findings
- **Hunten, Turco & Toon (1980).** Modelled meteoric ablation, which peaks near 84 km, and the coagulation of the evaporated material into "smoke" using a model adapted from stratospheric sulfate work. The suspended mass is mainly in smoke of radius 0.2 to 10 nm. Source: secondary summaries; the original paper was not read. — [GATS cosmic dust page](https://gwest.gats-inc.com/research/dust/cosmic_dust_page.html); [Megner et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-6-4415-2006)
- **Megner et al. (2006), ACP (downloaded).** Meteoric material ablates at 80 to 100 km and recondenses into smoke. Following Hunten et al., the model starts at a radius of 0.2 nm ("well within molecular dimensions") and uses Brownian coagulation, with eddy diffusion of 100 m²/s above 80 km. The two most important uncertain factors are coagulation efficiency (the sticking probability) and the background vertical wind. The results imply very few nanometre-sized smoke particles at the summer mesopause. Coagulation from differences in sedimentation speed and from turbulence is negligible for particles under 1 µm, so Brownian coagulation dominates. — [Megner et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-6-4415-2006)
- **Plane, Feng & Dawkins (2015), Chem. Rev. (downloaded).** MSPs form by polymerization of long-lived reservoir molecules such as FeOH, Mg(OH)2, NaHCO3 and SiO2. Electronic-structure calculations show highly exothermic routes to metal-silicate molecules, and these molecules polymerize via even more exothermic reactions, "hence, there should not be a free energy barrier associated with MSP formation". — [Plane et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1021/cr500501m)
- From the same review, the Saunders & Plane laboratory work: photo-oxidation of Fe, Mg and Si precursor vapors in a flow reactor gives particles of olivine-like composition. Coalescent growth produces primary particles of radius about 6 nm. Collisions between particles at or above that size form fractal-like aggregates. Fe-containing aggregates grow faster than hard-sphere coagulation predicts, which the authors attribute to magnetic dipole capture. Excess SiO2 forms separate amorphous silica particles that grow only by coalescence. — [Plane et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1021/cr500501m)
- Also from the review, observations: Arecibo incoherent-scatter radar gives MSP radii of 0.6 to 1.5 nm and number densities of 10² to 10⁴ cm−3 at 80 to 95 km. Embryonic Si-bearing MSPs of about 0.5 nm radius bind electrons strongly, by 2 to 3 eV. About 6 % of MSPs are negatively charged at 80 to 85 km. MSPs reach the surface 4 to 5 years after forming. Inside the Arctic polar vortex, up to 75 % of aerosol particles contain refractory material between 10 nm and a few micrometres. — [Plane et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1021/cr500501m)
- On models, the review reports that Bardeen et al. ran the first three-dimensional MSP simulations (WACCM with CARMA, a sectional aerosol model) using 21 size bins from 1 to 100 nm radius. Saunders et al. injected MSPs of 1.5 nm radius at 80 km, equivalent to 22 t/day of meteoric input. — [Plane et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1021/cr500501m)
- **Murphy et al. (2023), PNAS (downloaded).** Meteoric metals are oxidized below 85 km and condense into nanometre-sized smoke. About half of stratospheric sulfuric acid particles of 120 to 600 nm diameter contain a few weight per cent of meteoric metals and silicon. With the acid and water removed, that residue would be under 100 nm in diameter. — [Murphy et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313374120)
- **Plane et al. (2021), "Meteor-ablated aluminum in the mesosphere-lower thermosphere", JGR Space Physics (downloaded).** This is the first global model of meteoric aluminum (WACCM-Al). Al makes up about 1.4 % of cosmic dust mass, and the dust input is 28 ± 16 t/day. AlO, AlOH and Al+ are the major species above 80 km, and the AlO layer peaks at 89 km. — [Plane et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JA028792)
- The neutral scheme in Plane et al. (2021) is as follows:
  - Al + O2 → AlO + O, with k = 1.7 × 10−10 (T/300)^(1/6) cm³ s−1.
  - AlO adds O2 (with N2 as third body) to form OAlO2, and adds CO2 to form AlCO3.
  - OAlO2 and AlCO3 convert to AlOH via reaction with H (and with H2O through Al(OH)2).
  - AlOH, unlike FeOH, NaOH or CaOH, is stable against reaction with H and O and is therefore the main Al reservoir; only photolysis recycles it, with J(AlOH) ≈ 3.3 × 10−3 s−1.
  - Smoke formation is represented by a sink "polymerization" reaction, AlX + AlY → Al2XY with X, Y = O, OH or (OH)2.
  — [Plane et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1029/2020JA028792)

### Inferences
- The meteoric pathway is a strong template for a reentry model: ablation, then fast gas-phase oxidation (via AlO) to a hydroxide reservoir, then barrierless polymerization or dimerization, then coagulation to nanometre sizes, then fractal aggregation once primaries reach a few nanometres. The key difference is concentration. Meteoric input is about 28 t/day spread over the globe and the MLT region, whereas a single spacecraft releases up to tonnes along one trail. Reentry vapor is therefore orders of magnitude denser locally, and coagulation is faster. Reentry particles should be larger than meteoric smoke at the same age, unless the trail dilutes very quickly. Murphy et al. (2023) also invoke "mixing or coagulation in the cold far-field reentry plume" (Section 4).
- If aluminum condenses through AlOH or Al(OH)x reservoirs, as the Leeds chemistry and the SMASI result suggest (Section 4), the first particles may be hydroxide or oxyhydroxide and dehydrate to alumina later. This affects the assumed density, refractive index and surface chemistry.

### Gaps
- No laboratory measurement specific to aluminum smoke (as opposed to Fe/Mg/Si) was found. Saunders & Plane (2006, J. Atmos. Sol.-Terr. Phys.) is paywalled and was read only through the 2015 review.
- The sticking efficiency of the smallest Al-bearing clusters at mesospheric temperatures is unknown. Megner et al. show it is a first-order uncertainty.

## 4. Spacecraft and satellite reentry demise: what the literature assumes or finds about Al oxidation, alumina particle size and fraction condensed

### Takeaway
The reentry literature does not constrain alumina particle size or the fraction of aluminum that vaporizes. Inventories and climate models assume that all ablated aluminum becomes Al2O3 (an explicit upper bound), injected at about 60 to 70 km and condensed "somewhere" into particles. Aerosol models therefore test several size distributions as scenarios. The only size-resolved study (Ferreira et al. 2024, molecular dynamics) gives clusters of 0.4 to 4.2 nm aerodynamic diameter, but its method cannot represent vapor-phase nucleation and growth. It also finds most of the Al left unoxidized at 86 km, because oxygen supply is the limit. New laboratory results (SMASI, 2025) suggest that aluminum starts to evaporate only at about 1300 °C and that the main gas-phase product is aluminum hydroxide, not alumina. Observations confirm AlO emission at about 70 km in reentries, reentry metals inside stratospheric aerosol (about 10 % of particles larger than 120 nm), and a lidar-detected lithium plume at 96 km from a Falcon 9 upper stage.

### Cited Findings

**Observational evidence**
- **Murphy et al. (2023), PNAS (downloaded).** Single-particle mass spectrometry (PALMS) on the WB-57 aircraft at about 19 km detected more than 20 reentry-derived elements in stratospheric sulfuric acid particles, in alloy-consistent ratios. About 10 % of sulfuric acid particles larger than 120 nm contain Al and other reentry metals, and planned constellations could raise this to up to half. The measured Cu/Al mass ratio is 0.12 ± 0.06, against about 0.1 in reentry inventories. Some Li, Al, Cu and Pb comes from reentry in amounts exceeding the cosmic-dust input of those metals. — [Murphy et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313374120)
- Murphy et al. also give the following fluxes and interpretation:
  - Meteoroid Al influx is about 130 t/yr, of which about 20 t/yr ablates. About 210 t/yr of Al ablates from reentering spacecraft.
  - Up to 90 % of satellites and upper stages are vaporized.
  - Reentry metals "mix with and condense on meteoric smoke particles that are coming from above, followed by more coagulation during descent", with mixing or coagulation in the "cold far-field reentry plume".
  - A few particle spectra resemble solid-rocket alumina.
  - Their modelling used 0.4 Gg/yr of Al2O3 as a surrogate for present-day reentry emission.
  — [Murphy et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313374120)
- **Wing et al. (2026), Commun. Earth Environ. 7, 161 (downloaded).** A resonance lidar at Kühlungsborn measured a 10-fold enhancement of Li atoms at 96 km, about 20 hours after the uncontrolled reentry of a Falcon 9 upper stage on 19 February 2025. Back trajectories used UA-ICON winds. A Falcon 9 upper stage holds about 30 kg of Li in its AA2198 Al-Li tank walls. The authors expect most of that Li to vaporize well below 100 km, where it is "immediately oxidised to LiO and LiO2" and invisible to the lidar. This is the first observational evidence that ablation begins as high as 100 km. The Leeds Chemical Ablation Model was applied to the stage. — [Wing et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-025-03154-8)
- **AlO in reentry spectra.** Airborne spectroscopy of ATV-1 resolved Al at 395 nm and AlO bands at 460 to 520 nm just before the main explosion and break-up. Spectra of Cygnus OA6 (2016) showed AlO after ablation in the mesosphere. — [Ferreira et al. 2024, citing Lips et al. 2010 and Löhle et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024GL109280); the Stuttgart group places the Cygnus OA6 AlO detection at about 70 km — [Kuenstler et al., EGU26-1943](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-1943.html)
- **Leiser et al. (2025), Cygnus NG-20, 9th European Space Debris Conference (downloaded).** Cygnus NG-20 was observed from the air between 93 and 73 km. Spectra rose above background for 65 s, between 87 and 76 km. The main break-up was at about 76 km, typical for large spacecraft. — [Leiser et al. 2025](https://conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int/proceedings/sdc9/paper/206/SDC9-paper206.pdf)
- **Leiser, Loehle & Fasoulas (2022), J. Spacecraft Rockets 59, 1496 (downloaded, arXiv version).** Aluminum alloy samples under mechanical load in the PWK4 arc-jet plasma wind tunnel fail after distinct alkali-metal emission appears. Li lines mark the break-up of Al-Li alloys. — [Leiser et al. 2022, arXiv:2205.01090](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01090)

**Laboratory work specific to reentry**
- **SMASI (Gómez Martín, Mitchell, Ocaña & Plane), ESA workshop, September 2025 (abstract only).** SMASI is a successor to Leeds' MASI instrument. It ablates AA1421, AA6061 and AA7075 and measures two ablated species at once by laser-induced fluorescence, with mass spectrometry downstream. Reported findings:
  - Al starts to evaporate at about 1300 °C, controlled by its vapor pressure.
  - O2 slows Al evaporation only at concentrations above those near 60 km, and this depends on heating rate.
  - At 50 °C/s, O2 has little effect. At 4 °C/s, the oxide layer is clearly visible and Al is released in bursts as the shell cracks.
  - The main atmospheric product is aluminum hydroxide, "not alumina as is commonly assumed".
  - Li evaporates from about 800 °C and is nearly fully depleted before Al starts to evaporate.
  - The authors suggest that the high Al evaporation threshold may limit how much Al reaches the gas phase, given the relatively low apparent temperatures seen in real reentries.
  — [Gómez Martín et al., ESA 2025 contribution 11325](https://indico.esa.int/event/579/contributions/11325/)
- **Kuenstler et al. (EGU 2026).** "Molten or evaporated aluminum is assumed to oxidize almost immediately" because of abundant atomic O. Candidate products are AlO, Al(OH)x and solid Al2O3. Hydroxide and solid Al2O3 formation "have not been well tested experimentally". The group is building set-ups to detect solid particles. — [EGU26-1943](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-1943.html)
- **Beck & Young (2025, UK Space Agency, ESA workshop abstract).** A significant share of a spacecraft's aluminum could vaporize and be deposited as nanometre-sized alumina, but the effect "could, potentially, be relatively small". The key factor is the catalycity of the particle surface: low-catalycity Al2O3 heats much less than the high-catalycity Al seen in wind tunnels. Their analysis assumes that vaporization of the Al2O3 outer layer dominates, so any metallic Al vaporization would add to it. They assume all droplets reach the free stream, and it is unclear how many escape the parent body's wake. — [Beck & Young, ESA 2025 contribution 11327](https://indico.esa.int/event/579/contributions/11327/)

**Molecular dynamics**
- **Ferreira, Huang, Nomura & Wang (2024), GRL 51, e2024GL109280 (downloaded).** Setup:
  - ReaxFF molecular dynamics (Hong & van Duin 2015 Al–O force field) of an Al(111) fcc slab held at 2200 K, struck by O2 at 2 km/s normal to the surface.
  - 960 to 1360 Al atoms and 100 to 300 O atoms in nanometre-scale periodic boxes (the large case is 4.9 × 2.5 nm² in cross-section).
  - Represents a 250 kg satellite with 30 % Al by mass, a worst case of 90 s at 86 km (O2 number density 3.0 × 10^19 m−3), and an oxygen-to-aluminum molar ratio of 0.130 at full scale.
  - Atomic O is not included; O2 is taken as the relevant oxidant.
  — [Ferreira et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024GL109280)
- Ferreira et al. results:
  - Oxygen is fully consumed (an oxygen-limited regime). 436 Al and 176 O atoms form AlO clusters and 924 Al atoms remain as pure Al clusters.
  - At full scale, a 250 kg satellite yields about 29.8 kg of "AlO particles" plus 51.0 kg of unoxidized Al. Only 24.0 kg of Al is oxidized, leaving 68 % of the Al mass unoxidized.
  - Cluster aerodynamic diameters (unit-density spheres of the same mass) are 0.4 to 4.2 nm, with an expected full-scale value of 4.1 nm. Settling from 86 km to 40 km would take up to 30 years.
  - Totals: about 17 t of Al oxides from 2022 reentries (a 29.5 % increase in atmospheric Al above natural levels) and more than 360 t/yr in a mega-constellation scenario.
  - The authors state that coagulation in the mesosphere is "poorly understood" and recommend three-dimensional modelling.
  — [Ferreira et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024GL109280)

**Emission inventories and climate models**
- **Barker et al. (2024), Sci. Data 11, 1079 (downloaded).** "Al2O3 is the dominant metal oxide" from reentry ablation.
  - Assumed ablated fractions: 30 % for core stages and fairings, 65 % for upper stages, 80 % for non-megaconstellation payloads, 100 % for megaconstellation payloads. Reusable hardware does not ablate.
  - Assumed Al content: 70 % for rocket bodies, 40 % for payloads.
  - "We also assume that all ablated aluminium converts to Al2O3, thus yielding an upper estimate."
  - About 5 Gg of about 12 Gg of reentering mass returned unablated over 2020–2022.
  - The paper notes that knowledge and quantification of other products is lacking.
  — [Barker et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-024-03910-z)
- **Schulz & Glassmeier (2021), Adv. Space Res. 67, 1002 (downloaded, arXiv version).** Anthropogenic injection of Al was 211 t/yr in their present-day estimate, the largest element, and 807 t/yr in their constellation scenario. Meteoric Al influx is 131 t/yr. For meteoroids they assume ablated material enters the atmosphere in atomic form, because recondensation to dust is unlikely at small particle masses. — [Schulz & Glassmeier 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asr.2020.10.036); [arXiv:2008.13032](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13032)
- **Maloney, Portmann, Ross & Rosenlof (2025), JGR Atmospheres, doi 10.1029/2024JD042442 (paper not obtained; abstracts read).**
  - Model: WACCM6 coupled to the CARMA sectional aerosol model, with 10 Gg/yr of Al2O3 (consistent with about 60,000 satellites by 2040) injected at 60 to 70 km. Reentering satellites "completely vaporize in the mesosphere", and the vapor "will cool and coalesce into metallic aerosol roughly between 60 km and 70 km".
  - Multiple size distributions and reentry latitudes were tested.
  - A 20 to 40 Gg alumina burden accumulates poleward of 30° between 10 and 30 km within 1 to 2 years. This gives temperature anomalies of up to 1.5 K and a change of up to about 10 % in Southern Hemisphere polar-vortex wind speed.
  - Stratospheric chemistry is not included.
  — [Maloney et al., ESA 2025 abstract](https://indico.esa.int/event/579/contributions/11339/); [EGU25-3866](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-3866.html); DOI [10.1029/2024JD042442](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024JD042442)
- **Bernlochner et al. (EGU 2026).** Radiative transfer calculations using two size scenarios from Maloney et al. (2025) find that even the sign of the radiative forcing is uncertain because of "limited constraints on particle size distributions". — [EGU26-7078](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-7078.html)
- **Jain (2023), MIT thesis (Earth system model).** Simulations with fine alumina particles give 14 to 36 % larger radiative forcing and 1.54 times longer lifetimes than simulations with coarse alumina emissions. — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/54a15014-cc6d-4c1f-965e-e0b7d6759075/content)
- **Boley & Byers (2021), Sci. Rep. 11, 10642 (downloaded).** Starlink reentries alone could deposit more aluminum in the upper atmosphere than meteoroids do, because satellites are mostly Al while most meteoroids contain under 1 % Al by mass (against 54 t/day of meteoroid mass). - **Earlier studies cited by Ferreira et al.**
  - Park, Navarro-Laboulais, Leyland & Mischler (2021, Acta Astronautica 179, 604) used Gibbs-energy minimization to estimate by-products during reentry; Bianchi et al. (2021, ESA Clean Space Industrial Days) used it to estimate Al and Ti oxides.
  - Bekki et al. (2021, ESA Clean Space Industrial Days) used non-equilibrium CFD to predict NOx and chlorine.

  - According to Ferreira et al., none of these resolved particle size.

### Inferences
- **Ferreira et al.'s 0.4 to 4.2 nm "particle size" should not be used as a condensation result.** In my reading, the cluster sizes are bounded by the nanometre-scale periodic box, which holds about 1500 atoms, so a "massive" cluster of 831 atoms is simply most of the box. The run lasts picoseconds, so there is no time for vapor transport, nucleation, coagulation or cooling. The slab is held at 2200 K, below Al's normal boiling point (about 2790 K, a standard value), so vaporization is not represented. Atomic O is omitted although it is a major oxidant at 86 km. The diameters are "aerodynamic" (unit density), so the geometric sizes would be smaller still.
- What is useful from Ferreira et al. is the oxygen-limited stoichiometry: an O/Al molar ratio of 0.13 means oxidation is limited by oxygen supply at the surface of a large body. Supply of O and O2 to the vapor in the wake will likewise limit how fast and how completely the vapor oxidizes.
- **Consensus assumption versus emerging evidence.** Inventories (Barker, Murphy's 0.4 Gg/yr, Maloney's 10 Gg/yr) take "all ablated Al becomes Al2O3" as an upper bound. SMASI (aluminum hydroxide as the main product; Al evaporation only above about 1300 °C) and Beck & Young (catalycity may limit vaporization) both point to a smaller vapor fraction and a different chemical form. The meteoric chemistry of Plane et al. (2021) independently makes AlOH the stable reservoir. A model should therefore keep three things as separate, uncertain parameters: the fraction of ablated Al released as vapor versus as droplets, the oxidation pathway (AlO, then OAlO or OAlO2, then AlOH or Al2O3), and the condensation and coagulation step.
- **No paper predicts a nucleated size distribution.** Climate-model size distributions (Maloney, Bernlochner, Jain) are scenarios, not predictions. This is exactly the gap the user's model would fill, and the radiative and lifetime results show that size is a first-order control on the climate answer.
- **Altitude bracket.** Observed break-up is at about 73 to 76 km, AlO emission at about 70 km and Li release from about 100 km, and models inject at 60 to 70 km. Together these support a vapor-release window of roughly 60 to 100 km. Ambient O-atom and O2 abundances vary strongly across that window, so the oxidation pathway may change with release altitude.

### Gaps
- The Maloney et al. (2025) full text could not be downloaded: the NOAA repository returned "Access Denied" and Wiley blocked automated download. The exact size distributions tested (likely log-normal modes with stated median radii) are therefore not recorded here and should be read from the paper.
- I found no specific content for "Shutler et al." or "Prieto et al." on reentry alumina within the search budget. Ryan et al. (2022, Earth's Future, doi 10.1029/2021EF002612) is open access, but its PDF was blocked to automated download, so its reentry Al2O3 assumptions (including any assumed particle size) were not verified.
- I found no measurement of the size of reentry-derived particles at their formation altitude. Murphy et al. measure only particles larger than 120 nm in the stratosphere, after years of processing.
- Park et al. (2021) and the ESA ATMOS or Clean Space reports (Bekki, Bianchi, Lips 2021) were not read in full, so their equilibrium product fractions (Al2O3 versus AlO versus Al(g)) are not recorded.

## 5. Solid rocket motor (SRM) exhaust alumina: the submicron smoke mode, and how afterburning and quench set size

### Takeaway
SRM alumina is multimodal. Aircraft sampling of Space Shuttle and Titan IV plumes found modes near 0.005, 0.09 and 2.03 µm, with the smallest mode holding under 0.05 % of the mass. Athena II measurements found submicron particles to carry 8 % of the mass and 37 % of the surface area. The mass is dominated by a mode above 1 µm that sediments quickly, while a lesser submicron "smoke" mode can persist in the stratosphere and collect sulfuric acid. The submicron fraction is disputed between studies, and ground tests (the ESA EMAP project) were set up because the size distribution is "essentially unclear".

### Cited Findings
- **Ross et al. (1999).** In-situ stratospheric sampling of the plumes of two Space Shuttle SRMs and one Titan IV over 0.01 to 4 µm found a trimodal distribution with modes near 0.005, 0.09 and 2.03 µm. The smallest mode contains under 0.05 % of the alumina mass, well below earlier estimates of up to 10 %, which makes heterogeneous chlorine activation on that mode globally unimportant. The full citation is likely Ross, Whitefield, Hagen & Hopkins (1999), GRL 26, 819; this was not checked against the paper. — [Missouri S&T Scholars' Mine record](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/chem_facwork/297)
- **Schmid et al. (2003), JGR Atmospheres, doi 10.1029/2002JD002486.** Measured 0.004 to 1.2 µm in the Athena II plume: submicron particles carry 37 % of total surface area and 8 % of mass, higher than most earlier studies. The authors suggest submicron mass and surface fractions, and hence ozone loss, may be inversely related to motor thrust. — [UCAR record](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/size-resolved-particle-emission-indices-in-the-stratospheric-plum/); citation confirmed in [Murphy et al. 2023 reference 16](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313374120)
- **Atlas IIAS, Delta II and Athena II plume sampling (Missouri S&T).** Found a 1.1 µm mode consistent with spherical alumina, plus evidence of condensed volatiles that may be sampling artifacts. — [Scholars' Mine record](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/chem_facwork/290)
- **Murphy et al. (2023).** "The vast majority of the mass of alumina particles from solid rocket motor exhaust is contained in a >1 µm mode that quickly sediments from the stratosphere. A lesser submicron mode could remain in the stratosphere with subsequent condensation of sulfuric acid." A few stratospheric particles resemble SRM alumina analysed by Cziczo et al. (2002, GRL 29). — [Murphy et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313374120)
- **ESA EMAP (Saile et al. 2020, CEAS Space Journal).** A sub-scale SRM ground test in a wind tunnel, with schlieren imaging, spectroscopy and an aerodynamic particle sizer, set up because the left-behind particle size distribution is "essentially unclear" and Ross et al. versus Schmid et al. disagree. — [Saile et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12567-020-00338-0); [Polimi open-access copy](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/e0c31c10-537f-4599-e053-1705fe0aef77/SAILD01-20.pdf)
- **Barker et al. (2024).** Uses a primary Al2O3 emission index of 328 g per kg of solid propellant. Afterburning does not change the Al2O3 emission index; it changes only black carbon, NOx and other species. — [Barker et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-024-03910-z)

### Inferences
- The SRM smoke mode near 0.005 and 0.09 µm (5 nm and 90 nm) is the closest engineered analogue to vapor-derived alumina formed in a hot, high-pressure environment and then frozen by expansion. Its small mass fraction means vapor-phase alumina is a minor product when most aluminum burns as large droplets that leave oxide residues. The same split may apply to reentry, between droplets shed from the melt and vapor actually released. This is my inference: the formation mechanism of the SRM smoke mode (vapor-phase combustion and condensation in the chamber, versus droplet residue) is covered in the combustion literature and was not verified here.
- In an SRM, size is set mainly in the chamber and nozzle, at tens of atmospheres. Plume afterburning re-heats the gas but, per Barker et al., does not change the alumina emission index. The SRM size distribution therefore reflects much higher pressures than reentry and is an upper-bound analogue for growth by coagulation.

### Gaps
- Danilin et al. (2001, JGR) and Jackman et al. (1998), which assumed SRM alumina size distributions for global ozone modelling, were not retrieved. Their assumed distributions are not recorded here.
- No source tied the SRM smoke mode size quantitatively to nozzle quench rate.
