# Trail observations: when do eddies start to act on a thin trail at 50-110 km?

Scope note: about 17 tool calls. Primary text was read in full only for Lehmacher et al. 2011 (Annales Geophysicae) and Justus 1965 (Georgia Tech/NASA report, OCR text). Everything else comes from abstracts or search summaries and is flagged as such. I could not retrieve Booker and Cohen 1956, Booker 1956, Greenhow and Neufeld, Kaiser, Hawkins, Liller and Whipple, Zimmerman and Champion, Rosenberg, Blamont, Bedinger, Layzer, Larson, Beech, Yee, Ceplecha 1998 or Plane 2003/2012 at all; these are listed as gaps. Nearly all direct evidence below is at 86-110 km; nothing found is from 50-85 km.

## Meteor trains: altitudes of turbulent versus laminar behaviour, onset size and age

### Takeaway
The best quantitative train result found (Kelley et al. 2003) says Leonid trains at mesospheric heights spread with eddy diffusivities of several hundred m^2/s, about 100 times molecular, but the abstract gives no onset radius or age. Persistent trains form mostly at about 90 km, so the evidence is at 80-100 km only.

### Cited Findings
- Kelley, Kruschwitz, Gardner, Drummond and Kane (JGR 108(D8), 2003, doi 10.1029/2002JD002392) used persistent Leonid trains seen at Starfire Optical Range in 1998 and 1999. In three of four trains, parts showed molecular diffusion and the rest, plus the whole fourth train, showed eddy diffusion. Eddy diffusion coefficients were "several hundred m^2/s", two orders of magnitude above molecular. Sodium in the trains was a passive tracer; spectra fit the Heisenberg turbulence model; energy dissipation agreed with the eddy diffusion estimates; lidar showed convective and dynamic instability regions. The abstract gives no altitudes, inner scale or radius at onset. — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mesospheric-turbulence-measurements-from-persistent-leonid-meteor/)
- Jenniskens, Nugent and Plane (Earth, Moon and Planets, 2000): a tubular Leonid train expanded at about 10.5 m/s, roughly constant with altitude over 86-97 km, and the structures stayed intact for nearly 200 s. — [science.gov listing](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/l/leonid+persistent+train)
- Jenniskens, Rairden and DeVincenzi (2000): the 1999 "Y2K" train bent into a "2" within 1 minute and lasted at least 13 minutes; the shape is attributed to gravity-wave winds (scale height 8.3 km) at 79-91 km, not to turbulence. — [NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20010003444)
- Chu et al. (1998 Leonids, iron lidar): mean trail altitude 95.67 +/- 0.93 km, mean trail age 10.1 minutes. — [science.gov listing](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/l/leonid+persistent+train)
- A 2024 survey is reported to find meteors reaching 90 km far more likely to leave trains than those ending higher. — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/?p=3141914)
- Justus (1965) states that observations of intensity and duration of ambient turbulence on chemical releases and meteor trails rule out rocket- or meteor-generated wake turbulence as the origin of small-scale irregularities. — [NASA NTRS 19660004282](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19660004282/downloads/19660004282.pdf)

### Inferences
- Eddy diffusivity of several hundred m^2/s is huge compared with molecular diffusivity at 90 km (order 1-10 m^2/s), so trains at 86-97 km are turbulent over much of their lifetime; this contradicts a rule that eddies matter only above a 30 m radius, but the abstract does not give the radius at which it begins.
- The Y2K and tubular-train shapes are wind and shear effects, so shape alone is not evidence of eddy onset.

### Gaps
- Radius and age at onset of turbulence in meteor trains (Booker and Cohen 1956; Booker 1956; Hawkins; Liller and Whipple; Greenhow and Neufeld; Kaiser; Zimmerman; Rosinski and Snow; Beech; Yee): not retrievable here. Only bibliographic confirmation that Booker and Cohen is JGR 61(4), 707 (1956) ([scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/a0cd0884631658fa3d6f33398e480f69)).
- Kelley 2003 inner scale, altitudes, dissipation rate: full text not read.

## Chemical releases: breakup time and size, regime laws, inner scale

### Takeaway
Chemical trails at 87-103 km become globular (turbulent) within tens of seconds at diameters of tens to hundreds of metres. Rees et al. 1972 (as quoted by Lehmacher 2011) gives the sharpest answer: onset of turbulent expansion 10-20 s after release at trail diameters of only 40-60 m, near 88-93 km. That is a radius of 20-30 m, so it is close to, not far below, the model's 30 m.

### Cited Findings
- Visual turbopause: chemical trails show a transition from three-dimensional turbulent structure to laminar flow within a few hundred metres of altitude or less, often near 103 km (Blamont 1963). All eight TMA trails in the 2009 Turbopause experiment (Poker Flat) were turbulent up to about 102-103 km, with the globular-to-laminar transition at 101, 102 or 103 km, varying little over two hours. — [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)
- Justus (1965): globule cutoff altitude on 21 chemical releases averaged 106 km +/- 4 km rms; globules become smaller with decreasing altitude and below about 90 km clouds lose their spherical globule shape and look "fuzzy". Turbulence cannot exist above about 106 km by an energy balance argument. — [Justus 1965](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19660004282/downloads/19660004282.pdf)
- Justus (1965) globule growth: very rapid expansion during the first 150 +/- 40 s after release, then almost no growth for 50 +/- 10 s (matching the time scale of the largest buoyancy-subrange eddies), then growth with d^2 scaling as t^5 (Bolgiano buoyancy-subrange law) in the OCR text; the level-phase diameter is nearly constant between 98 and 106 km. Maximum buoyancy-subrange eddy size about 0.8 km at 98 km to the turbopause; turbulent mixing length about 0.75 km below the turbopause; maximum vertical scale of turbulent winds about 7 km and horizontal about 10 km at 100 km; dissipation near 0.4 and 0.35 W/kg between 90 km and the turbopause (the OCR text is garbled on which quantities these are). Globule expansion rarely measurable beyond about 3 km diameter. Smallest globules in his data about 200 m diameter (camera limited); Blamont and de Jager (1961) saw globules as small as 90 m diameter. — [Justus 1965](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19660004282/downloads/19660004282.pdf)
- Regime laws as described by Lehmacher et al. (citing Rees et al. 1972): after the first few seconds of release-driven motion, the trail expands by molecular diffusion, then inertial-range turbulence appears as a faster expansion with r^2 proportional to t^3 for a limited time. Rees et al. derived dissipation rates of 40-120 mW/kg from onset time and 370 mW/kg from the t^3 slope, and observed onset of turbulent expansion 10-20 s after release at diameters of 40-60 m, giving dissipation of 10-20 mW/kg in the 87-93 km region. — [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)
- Turbopause experiment (2009, four rockets, TMA, 8 trails): trail width 300 m at 88 km and 600 m at 92 km at 40 s after release, then roughly constant; authors judge the trail already in break-up phase and dissipation estimates from it too high (1500 mW/kg for 300 to 600 m in 30 s; 250 mW/kg for 500 to 600 m over 40 s). Turbulence evidence: sodium mixing near 75 km, density fluctuations and dissipation at 87-90 km, fast irregular expansion at 90-93 km and 95-103 km; mainly laminar above 103 km, with vortices up to 118 km; above 118 km smooth rapid molecular-diffusion expansion. Trail turbulence matched regions of near-adiabatic lapse rate and Richardson number below 1. — [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)
- Other quoted values: Bishop et al. 2004 (TOMEX) dissipation 130 mW/kg near 87 km from TMA expansion, and 340 and 840 mW/kg at 100 and 98 km with kilometre-scale trail irregularities; the 12-flight high-latitude winter mean from density fluctuations is 7.2-21.8 mW/kg at 80-100 km (Luebken 1997). — [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)
- Chu, Su, Larsen and Chao (2007, JGR, Taiwan TMA release): turbulent structures in much of the trail below 110 km, peak wind 154 m/s near 105 km; structures about 1.8 km apart in shear layers 0.7-1.5 km deep, read as counter-rotating vortices in a breaking gravity wave. — [Bohrium summary](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/53e9bcd9b7602d970495c159), [EarthRef](https://earthref.org/ERR/98606)
- Roper (1996, JGR 101, 7013) reinterpreted a 1969 Skylark TMA trail over Woomera in terms of molecular and turbulent diffusion and gravity-wave scales (abstract only). — [EarthRef](https://earthref.org/ERR/51955)
- Hecht et al. 2004 (TOMEX): Kelvin-Helmholtz billows, enhanced dissipation and mixed regions in unstable layers in 80-105 km. — [ERAU](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/an-overview-of-observations-of-unstable-layers-during-the-turbule/)

### Inferences
- Rees et al.'s 40-60 m diameter at 10-20 s is the one number found that bears directly on the user's question; it implies onset radius of about 20-30 m at roughly 88-93 km, consistent with the model's 30 m (21-42 m) rather than far below it. It is a second-hand quote and for TMA releases that start with a large initial puff, not a thin wake.
- The 300-600 m widths at 40 s are release-puff widths and say nothing about the smallest eddy.

### Gaps
- Original Rees et al. 1972, Blamont and de Jager 1961, Rosenberg, Bedinger, Layzer, Larson, Zimmerman and Champion: not found. A search for Zimmerman and Champion returned nothing relevant.
- No chemical-release evidence at 50-85 km except the sodium mixed layer near 75 km (Lehmacher).
- Justus d^2 and t^5 relation is from garbled OCR; check the original before use.

## Rocket and shuttle plumes, reentry trails

### Takeaway
Little direct evidence on small-scale spreading. Shuttle plumes at 100-115 km spread diffusively at about 23 m/s over 24 h, and molecular diffusion is said to dominate there. The Falcon 9 lithium event gives layer thickness but no radius against time.

### Cited Findings
- 27 shuttle plumes (2002-2011) from satellite water-vapour data: deposited at 100-115 km, mean diffusive spreading about 23 m/s over 24 h, superimposed on advective transport (units as stated in the search summary). — [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/196546)
- Stevens et al. 2005: between 100 and 115 km, where most shuttle plume was injected, molecular diffusion dominates over eddy diffusion; iron lidar saw shuttle-engine iron a day after a January 2003 launch. — [Stevens et al. 2005 GRL](https://cires1.colorado.edu/science/groups/chu/pubs/documents/2005GRL_Stevens_AntarcticShuttlePMCFe.pdf)
- Wing et al. (Communications Earth and Environment, February 2026): Falcon 9 upper stage reentered 19 February 2025 over the Atlantic west of Ireland; lithium lidar detection about 20 h later over Germany, layer at 94-97 km, peak about 31 atoms/cm^3 versus about 3 before, lasting about 27 minutes; about 30 kg lithium per stage. Press-summary sourced; breakup location differs between reports. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/spacex_falcon_europe_breakup_lithium_plume/)
- ECOMA09 rockets: strong turbulence 86-89 km with dissipation maximum about 2 W/kg. — search summary only.

### Gaps
- No Kelley or Stevens paper found giving plume radius against time or an inner scale; nothing on reentry wakes at 50-90 km; nothing from the Wing paper on early spreading.

## Early trail expansion: initial radii

### Takeaway
Radar-derived initial radii are about 0.5-2 m at 85-95 km, well above the model's 8 mm start, rising by roughly a factor of two per 10-12 km.

### Cited Findings
- Radius evolves as a^2 = r0^2 + 4Dt with D the ambipolar diffusion coefficient. — [Stober/arXiv 2108.07647](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.07647)
- CMOR 2018: 0.5-2 m at 85-95 km, mostly 1-1.5 m, about twice earlier values; parallel echoes 0.25-1.5 m. Greenhow and Hall (1960): about 1 m at 90 km to about 3 m at 115 km. Kashcheyev and Lebedinets (1963): about 0.8 m near 90 km. Jones and Campbell-Brown (2005): doubles every 10-12 km. Baggaley (1970): r proportional to density^-0.45 and speed^-0.57. — same source.
- Booker-style ambipolar radii for trains and the 20 cm start: not found.

### Gaps
- Initial radii below 85 km, and optical train widths, not found.

## Smallest scale stated directly for turbulence acting on trails

### Takeaway
No paper found states a single minimum scale. The nearest statements are inner-scale scalings and the Rees onset diameter.

### Cited Findings
- Inner scale l0 = 9.9 (nu^3/epsilon)^(1/4), growing roughly as density^(-3/4). For a large dissipation of 100 mW/kg it is about 4, 10, 40, 100 and 400 m at 70, 80, 90, 100 and 110 km. Expected inner scales are 100 m or more at 100 km and 10 m or more at 80 km. — [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)
- Below 95 km in that flight no inertial range or inner scale could be identified in the 95-103 km region (fluctuation scales were several hundred metres). — same source.
- Rees onset diameter 40-60 m (above). Smallest observed globules 90-200 m (Justus).

### Inferences
- With typical dissipation (10-20 mW/kg, lower than 100) the inner scale is larger than the values above, so 30 m radius (60 m diameter) near 85-90 km is plausible; at 50-70 km the inner scale shrinks to a few metres or less, so the model's constant 30 m is probably too large there but this is a scaling argument, not an observation.

### Gaps
- No observation below 75 km; no paper states the smallest scale outright.
