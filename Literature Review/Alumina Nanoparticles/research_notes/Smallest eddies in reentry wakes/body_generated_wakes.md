# Body-generated wakes of re-entering droplets, fragments and the parent at 50 to 90 km: do they turbulize, and how small are their eddies?

Date of research: 2026-11-05. Scope: molten AA7075 droplets (1 to 200 micrometres), a fragment of about 10 centimetres and a 3U CubeSat-class parent, at 50 to 90 km altitude and 2 to 8 km/s. This file builds on `citations/wake_radius_growth_laws.md` and `citations/laminar_wake_growth_laws.md` and does not repeat them; where it reuses one of their findings it says so.

How to read the labels. "Read in full" means I read the paper text itself (the PDFs in `Literature Review/Wake Growth/` or an arXiv PDF). "Snippet only" means I saw only a search-engine summary or an abstract, so the claim is second-hand. "Calculation" means my own arithmetic, done in Python on 2026-11-05 with an atmosphere built from the US Standard Atmosphere 1976 layer equations (my pressures and densities reproduce the published table at 70 and 80 km: 5.221 pascals and 8.283e-5 kilograms per cubic metre at 70 km; 1.052 pascals and 1.846e-5 at 80 km; the 90 km values 0.1836 pascals and 3.416e-6 kilograms per cubic metre are the published table values, taken directly). Viscosity is from Sutherland's law (1.458e-6 times T^1.5 divided by T plus 110.4, T in kelvin), and the mean free path (the average distance a molecule travels between collisions) is viscosity divided by 0.499 times density times mean molecular speed. Knudsen number is always the mean free path divided by body DIAMETER (not radius). Reynolds number is always freestream density times speed times diameter divided by freestream viscosity. All drag coefficients used in calculations are assumptions: 1.0 for fragments and the parent, 2.0 for droplets in transitional or free-molecular flow.

The short answer, stated up front. By the two standard transition criteria (Lees 1964 and the Goldburg pressure-diameter correlation quoted by Taylor et al. 1964), a wake behind a body only turns turbulent when the body is large and the air is not too thin. For a 10 cm fragment or a 3U-CubeSat-sized parent, the wake is laminar at 60 to 90 km at every speed from 2 to 8 km/s, and is at best marginal at 50 km. For 200 micrometre and 1 mm droplets, no continuum wake exists at all, let alone a turbulent one. A turbulent wake appears only for bodies of about 30 cm and larger below roughly 55 to 60 km, and for metre-class bodies below roughly 65 to 70 km. So "the smallest eddies in this wake" has no turbulent answer for the droplet or the fragment: there is no body-generated cascade, and the only turbulence acting on the trail is the background atmospheric turbulence. If a turbulent wake did exist (a body of 30 cm to 3 m at the altitudes given), its smallest eddies (the Kolmogorov scale) would be 1 to 100 millimetres, they would live for seconds to a few minutes, and they would be dead long before the hours-long plume evolution begins.

## 1. What is the hypersonic wake transition criterion, and what do the 1960s data say?

### Takeaway
Wake turbulence behind a blunt body at hypersonic speed requires a freestream Reynolds number (based on body diameter) above a critical minimum of about 5 times 10 to the fourth (50,000) at about 6 km/s; equivalently a pressure-times-drag-length product above about 0.5 to 2 centimetres of mercury times centimetres. Below that the wake stays laminar however far downstream one looks; for the sizes and altitudes of this problem that means laminar for everything below roughly a 30 cm body above about 60 km.

### Cited Findings
- Lees (1964) states that transition to turbulence in the viscous inner wake downstream of the neck occurs only if the Reynolds number based on conditions ahead of the body exceeds a critical value, "about 5 times 10 to the fourth at a velocity of the order of 20,000 feet per second [6.1 km/s], corresponding to an altitude of about 200,000 feet [61 km] for a blunt body with a nose diameter of 1 foot [0.3 m]." — [Lees 1964, AIAA J. 2(3), 417-428, read in full from `Literature Review/Wake Growth/lees-2012-hypersonic-wakes-and-trails.pdf`](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356)
- Lees (1964) also estimates a minimum critical Reynolds number below which wake turbulence "cannot maintain itself against the action of viscous dissipation", reached "roughly" when the effective turbulent diffusivity falls below the laminar diffusivity. The estimate for a blunt body at Mach 20 is (freestream Reynolds number on diameter) minimum of about 5 times 10 to the fourth, corresponding to about 200,000 feet (61 km) for a 6 foot (1.8 m) body; for a sharp slender body at Mach 20 it is about 3 times 10 to the fourth. The experimental minimum local wake Reynolds number from cylinder data (Kendall; Behrens) is about 1500 against the theoretical 500. — [Lees 1964, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356)
- Lees's "tentative picture": below the minimum critical Reynolds number the wake is laminar; just above it, transition first appears about 40 to 50 body diameters behind a blunt body and then moves forward as ambient pressure increases, holding a constant Reynolds number based on local distance and conditions at the turbulent front of 5.6 times 10 to the fourth, independent of body diameter; when it reaches the neck it sticks until the shear layer itself transitions. The paper adds that transition in a blunt-body wake "cannot occur aft of" about 40 diameters at the minimum Reynolds number. — [Lees 1964, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356); restated in [Lykoudis 1966, AIAA J. 4(4), 577-590, read in full from `lykoudis-2012-a-review-of-hypersonic-wake-studies.pdf`](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493)
- Lees cites radar observations by Lin of a turbulent wake behind Colonel Glenn's capsule during re-entry at 220,000 feet (67 km) as agreeing with this minimum (6 foot body). A search result describing the Lin paper itself puts the capsule at about 230,000 feet (70 km) and says the wake's radar backscatter cross-section was several thousand times that of the bare body. — [Lees 1964, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356); altitude and cross-section from a [NASA Technical Reports Server listing, search snippet only](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19690009638/downloads/19690009638.pdf)
- Lykoudis (1966) reviews how transition is correlated: in free flight it is identified visually as the distance behind the body at which roughness first appears at the edge of the viscous core; Webb, Hromas and Lees showed theoretically that the local wake Reynolds number scales as the freestream Reynolds number to the power of minus one quarter times pressure times diameter, so "stability depends essentially on the product of ambient pressure and diameter alone", a conclusion also reached experimentally by Slattery and Clay, Demetriades and Gold, and Hidalgo et al.; for blunt bodies the Reynolds number based on transition distance and conditions at the turbulent front is 5.6 times 10 to the fourth, and about four times higher for sharp bodies. — [Lykoudis 1966, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493)
- Contradictory data: Clay, Labitt and Slattery indicated transition beyond 1000 diameters at Mach numbers near 17, but Wilson's ballistic-range spheres near Mach 18 contradicted those data, and Lykoudis says "their statistical significance should be scrutinized". Lykoudis also notes that exact transition numbers "have yet to be settled". — [Lykoudis 1966, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493)
- Taylor, Melcher and Washburn (1964) use Goldburg's pressure-based correlation (transition at a shoulder Reynolds number of 6000): the wake is turbulent when the product of ambient pressure and drag length, p times the square root of (drag coefficient times frontal area), exceeds 2.0 centimetres of mercury times centimetres, transitional between 0.5 and 2.0, laminar below 0.5. Data: 0.22, 0.55 and 1.0 inch spheres at 3.7 to 6.2 km/s and 0.2 to 10 centimetres of mercury (about 270 to 13,000 pascals). — [Taylor et al. 1964, AIAA J. 2(10), 1731-1738, read in full; summarised in `citations/wake_radius_growth_laws.md` section 0](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2656)
- Pallone, Erdos and Eckerman (1964) correlate transition for slender cones as a wake Reynolds number (using the local velocity defect and wake diameter) against a defect Mach number: about 10 to the third to 10 to the fourth at defect Mach number 1 to 2, rising to about 10 to the fifth at 6 to 8, read off a figure with large scatter. — Pallone et al. 1964, AIAA J. 2, 855-863, read in full and summarised in `citations/laminar_wake_growth_laws.md` section 3 (local PDF `Literature Review/Wake Growth/pallone-et-al-2012-hypersonic-laminar-wakes-and-transition-studies.pdf`; no URL checked)
- Goldburg's AVCO-Everett summary organises ballistic-range transition data into a far-wake law, a near-wake law and an interpolation regime using a Reynolds number built from local gas conditions at transition, and the correlating constant is taken from ballistic-range data; in the high-Mach-number limit the Reynolds number reduces to pressure times a length; the pressure-based correlations are "good to a factor of 2 in the pressure". — [Goldburg report, DTIC AD0805554 (HTTP 403 on fetch; search-engine summary only)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0805554.pdf)
- Fay and Goldburg (1963) found a close analogy between unsteady hypersonic and incompressible sphere wakes, with the hypersonic wake's zero-intercept Reynolds number about 3000 against about 200 for the incompressible one (so the hypersonic wake needs a much higher Reynolds number before it becomes unsteady). — [search snippet quoting the paper, via arXiv 2408.02933, second-hand](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.02933)
- Cylinder data from Lees's students: Behrens (1968) found the inner viscous wake of cylinders at Mach 6 entirely laminar for Reynolds numbers below 10 to the fourth (range 200 to 4000); McCarthy (1962) covered Mach 5.7 at Reynolds numbers of 4300 to 66,500 and correlated transition with other data; Kubota and McCarthy's Mach 5.8 tunnel data cover Reynolds numbers 6000 to 80,000. These are cylinders, not spheres. — [Caltech thesis listing for Behrens, search snippet only](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20170731-154902517)
- At a modern benchmark, 25 millimetre spheres at Mach 10.5 (about 3.4 km/s) at conditions equivalent to 20 km altitude became turbulent 1.16 to 1.22 diameters behind the sphere, with large-scale turbulence first at 8.9 diameters (data to 53 diameters). The Reynolds number there is far above anything in this problem, so it shows only that transition moves to the neck at high pressure. — [Redmond et al. 2026, AIAA 2026-5068, abstract only](https://scholar.afit.edu/facpub/3584)
- Demetriades's hot-wire work (AIAA J. 2, 245-250, 1964) measured wind-tunnel density fluctuations of 0.10 to 0.40 (relative to the mean) in turbulent hypersonic wakes; Slattery and Clay's schlieren-based values for spheres were about 0.9 at 400 diameters, but after a numerical error was corrected by a factor of the square root of two the two agree reasonably. — [Lykoudis 1966, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493)
- Levensteins and Krumins (1967) report a ballistic-range programme on how transition and wake structure depend on Mach number, Reynolds number and wall enthalpy; I saw no numbers from it. — [AIAA J. 5(9), 1596, abstract only](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.4256)
- Lees and Hromas (1962) predict turbulent inner-wake growth that agrees with sphere shadowgraph widths from 200 to 4000 diameters downstream; their model stresses that turbulence is "initially strong" but I read no transition criterion from them. — [Lees and Hromas 1962, J. Aerospace Sci. 29(8), 976, abstract and DTIC AD0600632 snippet only](https://doi.org/10.2514/8.9671)

The conclusion to draw: at 60 km or above, only bodies of a metre or more can have turbulent wakes; a 10 cm fragment is at best marginal, at the very bottom of the altitude range, and only at the top speed.
- Cross-check against the earlier notes. My Reynolds number for the 2.8 metre generic spacecraft at 80 km and 8 km/s is 3.1 times 10 to the fourth, matching the 3.0 times 10 to the fourth in `wake_radius_growth_laws.md` section 0, and my Goldburg boundary of 74 km for it matches the 74 km quoted there. Applying Lees's rule to Glenn's capsule (1.9 m, 7.6 km/s, 70 km): Reynolds number 8.3 times 10 to the fourth, above 5 times 10 to the fourth, and pressure-diameter 0.66 (transitional), consistent with the radar observation of a turbulent wake.
- Calibration caveat. Both criteria were calibrated at about 3.7 to 6.3 km/s (Goldburg, Taylor) and about 6 km/s (Lees's Mach 20) in ballistic ranges and wind tunnels whose lowest static pressure is about 270 pascals (about 0.2 centimetres of mercury), while the pressures at 50 to 90 km are 80 to 0.18 pascals. The match is therefore only in Reynolds number or pressure-diameter product, not in absolute pressure, and the boundaries above are uncertain by several kilometres. Lees's own sharp-body minimum varies by a factor of six between Mach 5 and Mach 20, so extending the blunt-body value of 5 times 10 to the fourth to 2 km/s (Mach 6) is the weakest step in the 2 km/s column.
- Transition location if a wake is just above the threshold: about 40 to 50 diameters behind the body (4 to 5 metres for a 10 cm fragment, 12 to 15 metres for 30 cm), moving toward the body as pressure rises. The turbulent wake therefore starts essentially immediately at the scale of the fragment's own wake structure.

### Gaps
- Lees and Hromas 1962, Levensteins and Krumins 1967 and Goldburg's report (DTIC) were not read in full (DTIC refuses the fetch tool; the Hromas and Lees and Levensteins and Krumins numbers are therefore absent). Hromas and Lees' own transition numbers, and Demetriades's transition Reynolds number for spheres at Mach 8, could not be found in any accessible source.
- No transition data exist at the pressures of 50 to 90 km (below 80 pascals) for any body; every criterion above is an extrapolation in the sense that the Reynolds number is matched but the Knudsen number and the shock-layer physics are not (a body of 10 cm at 90 km has a Knudsen number of 0.2).
- I did not read the Slattery and Clay transition papers (Physics of Fluids 1961, 1962) beyond abstracts held in the earlier notes.

## 2. Freestream Reynolds number, Knudsen number and expected wake state for 200 micrometre, 1 mm, 10 cm and 30 cm bodies

### Takeaway
All 200 micrometre and 1 mm droplet cases have Reynolds numbers of at most 96 and 482 (and below 10 for most of the altitude range), Knudsen numbers from 0.07 to 99, and no continuum wake; 10 cm bodies have Reynolds numbers of 54 to 48,000 and are laminar except marginally at 50 km and 8 km/s; 30 cm bodies are turbulent only at 50 to 60 km and speeds above about 4 km/s.

### Cited Findings
- Flow-regime definitions used by the meteor community: free molecular flow for Knudsen number above 10, transitional between 0.1 and 10, slip flow between 0.01 and 0.1, continuum below 0.01; with the warning that meteoroid shock waves appear at lower Knudsen numbers than a classical estimate would suggest, because of the ablation-created vapour cap. — [Silber et al. 2018, Adv. Space Res. 62(3), 489-532, read in full (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07842)
- The same review states that for a meteoroid "if Re is at most 1" viscous forces matter and must be included, and that the Knudsen number inside the vapour cap is about the inverse of the Reynolds number times the ratio of the evaporated-atom speed to the sound speed (their equations 9 and 10). It also says that no comprehensive model yet describes the flow field and shock formation of a strongly ablating meteoroid in the non-continuum regime. — [Silber et al. 2018, read in full](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07842)
- Falcon 9 upper-stage re-entry (February 2025): meter-scale fragments seen optically between about 85 and 36 km with the detection peak near 60 km; radar echoes from 55 to 75 km; the authors state the Knudsen numbers of such fragments in that altitude range are well below one, consistent with continuum flow. Wake turbulence is not discussed in the abstract. — [arXiv 2605.29124, abstract read only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29124)

Reading the table: the droplets sit in the transitional and free-molecular regimes at every altitude above 50 km, with Reynolds numbers about a thousand times below the 50,000 needed for turbulence. The user's design assumption that droplet Reynolds numbers are "mostly below 10" is confirmed above 60 km, but at 50 km and 8 km/s a 200 micrometre droplet reaches 96 and a 1 mm one 482; these are still tens to hundreds of times too low for a turbulent wake. The Knudsen number figures also confirm the design note: 0.37 at 50 km for 200 micrometres and 99 at 90 km. A 10 cm fragment is in the continuum regime up to about 75 km and slip or transitional flow above it.

For the 3U CubeSat-class parent (about 10 by 10 by 34 cm), the equal-area diameter is about 0.1 to 0.2 metres, which falls between the 10 cm and 30 cm rows. Its wake is laminar everywhere in 60 to 90 km by both criteria, and marginal to laminar at 50 km. That conclusion differs from the 2.8 metre generic spacecraft treated in the earlier notes, which is turbulent below 75 to 77 km.

### Gaps
- Real fragments are not smooth spheres, tumble, and shed vapour and melt; surface roughness, ablation-gas injection and non-spherical shape lower the transition Reynolds number in boundary layers and may do the same in wakes, but I found no quantitative wake data for ablating or tumbling bodies at these Reynolds numbers.
- The speed of a real fragment at 50 km is not 8 km/s: a 10 cm object would have decelerated; the table is a map, not a trajectory.

## 3. Where a turbulent wake exists: width, decay time, velocity scale, dissipation rate, Kolmogorov scale and lifetime

### Takeaway
A turbulent wake exists in this study's range only for a 30 cm body at 50 to 60 km (at 4 km/s and above), for bodies of 1 m below about 65 km, and for a 10 cm fragment only at the edge at 50 km and 8 km/s. Where it exists its smallest eddies (Kolmogorov scale, calculated with the wake gas at ambient viscosity) are about 0.3 to 100 millimetres, depending on distance behind the body, its turbulence is energetic for roughly 1 to 12 seconds after passage (until the mean velocity defect falls to 10 metres per second), and it merges into background turbulence by 30 seconds to 6 minutes (defect 1 metre per second), a negligible fraction of the hours-long plume evolution.

### Cited Findings
- Width: the turbulent wake radius grows as the cube root of distance: w/(drag coefficient times area)^(1/2) equals 0.66 times (x/(drag coefficient times area)^(1/2)) to the power of one third, where w is the full luminous width and x the distance behind the body, fitted to ballistic-range data with root-mean-square deviation 23 percent for 2 to 1200 drag-length distances. — [Taylor et al. 1964, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2656)
- Park's version: wake boundary radius equals 0.7 times reference radius times (distance from a fictitious source divided by reference radius) to the one third; the law is cut off at 10 to the fifth feet (30.5 km) behind Park's vehicle, where the mean velocity in the wake has fallen to 30 feet per second (9.1 m/s), "the same order as the velocity of natural turbulence", after which dispersion goes as the square root of time (random walk) instead of the one third power. — [Park 1971, NASA TM X-62,052, read in full, summarised in `wake_radius_growth_laws.md` section 3](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19720024212/downloads/19720024212.pdf)
- Turbulence statistics: Lees models the effective turbulent diffusivity as a constant times the local velocity difference across the inner wake times the wake width, the constant being inversely proportional to Townsend's universal Reynolds number; turbulence intensity is proportional to the local velocity defect and its scale to the local wake width. — [Lees 1964, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356)
- Lykoudis: "there are no experiments yet available which can tell us something about the structure of turbulence behind hypersonic turbulent wakes", and Lykoudis recommends that compressible-wake turbulence be brought "to the same level of ignorance" as the incompressible case. Measured relative density fluctuations in turbulent hypersonic wakes are 0.1 to 0.9 (see section 1). — [Lykoudis 1966, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493)
- Late-time departure: in a towed-sphere water experiment (30 mm, Reynolds number 13,000) the cube-root growth held only from 20 to 50 diameters and switched to square-root growth from 75 to 145 diameters while still turbulent; thresholds quoted from the literature are a wake Reynolds number (integral width times centreline defect over viscosity) of about 500 at the switch (Johansson et al.), about 100 below which the inertial range vanishes (Uberoi and Freymuth), and of order 1 for relaminarisation (Tennekes and Lumley). — [Skidmore, Philip and Monty 2018, read in full, summarised in `wake_radius_growth_laws.md` section 4.7](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/imarusic/proceedings/21/Contribution_584_final.pdf)

### Inferences
Everything below is calculation, using these assumptions, which are mine and not from the sources: the Taylor width law with the wake radius taken as half of w and identified with the Gaussian one-over-e radius b (this is uncertain by perhaps 1.5 times); a Gaussian velocity defect whose momentum equals the drag (half density times speed squared times drag coefficient times area), which gives the centreline defect divided by flight speed equal to 1.46 times (distance divided by drag length) to the minus two thirds, so, for example, 0.29 at 11 drag lengths, 0.068 at 100 and 0.015 at 1000; drag coefficient 1; turbulent velocity fluctuation taken as 0.25 of the centreline defect (typical of incompressible axisymmetric wakes; not from any hypersonic source, since Lykoudis says none exist); dissipation rate taken as the turbulent velocity cubed divided by the wake radius (an order-of-magnitude estimate, good to perhaps a factor of 3); Kolmogorov scale equal to viscosity cubed over dissipation rate, to the one quarter, using ambient viscosity (lower bound; if the wake gas is about three times hotter, viscosity is about five times higher at ambient pressure and the scale is 3.3 times larger, which is the "hot" column). The cube-root law was fitted only to distances of 2 to 1200 drag lengths, so distances beyond about a thousand drag lengths are extrapolations (marked with an asterisk).

| | 1 km | 0.13 | 0.66 | 23 | 5.8 | 298 | 11 / 37 |

Cross-check with the published law: for the 1 m body at 6 km/s the Taylor-type radius at 30 km is 9.5 m and Park's reference law cuts off at a defect of about 9 m/s at 30 km for a 2.8 m, 7 km/s vehicle; my defect for the 2.8 m case at 30 km is about 20 m/s, so the two agree within a factor of about two, which is as good as the one-third-power coefficients agree with each other (a factor of 1.45 in radius, per the earlier notes).

Lifetime. Using the same formulas the centreline defect falls to 10 metres per second at 6.9 km (1.15 seconds) for the 30 cm case, 23 km (3.8 seconds) for the 1 m case at 60 km, and 81 km (11.6 seconds) for the 2.8 m case; and to 1 metre per second, taken as the background natural turbulence velocity (the same criterion Park uses), at 218 km (36 seconds), 727 km (121 seconds) and 2560 km (366 seconds). Beyond those times the wake is no longer a separately identifiable turbulent structure; it is part of the background. The wake Reynolds number (centreline defect times wake radius over viscosity) stays above 500, the Johansson threshold at which cube-root growth ends, until 35 seconds (30 cm case), 147 seconds (1 m case) and 314 seconds (2.8 m case), and falls to the 100 level (inertial range vanishing) only after 1.2 to 11 hours (4360 s, 18,400 s and 39,300 s), though that last number is a power-law extrapolation more than a thousand times beyond the range of the data and the true decay at that stage is controlled by the stratified atmosphere, not by the body. So the plain statement is: body-generated turbulence is energetic for seconds, identifiable for up to several minutes, and effectively gone well inside the first ten minutes, against hours for the plume. For the Re-entry of a 10 cm marginal fragment at 50 km the equivalent times are 0.44 seconds (10 metres per second) and 14 seconds (1 metre per second).

Where the Kolmogorov scale matters: the numbers say eddies from 1 to 100 millimetres exist at the most in the first tens of seconds in a wake of width 0.4 to 30 metres, so the trail's mixing is complete across the wake (turbulent turnover time wake radius over turbulent velocity is 0.004 s at 30 metres, 0.15 s at 1 kilometre, 1.5 s at 10 kilometres for the 30 cm case) long before diffusion by the background atmosphere (30 metre inner scale) could act.

### Gaps
- No measurement of the internal structure (spectrum, dissipation rate, small-scale cut-off) of a hypersonic turbulent wake was found; Lykoudis states none existed in 1966. Every dissipation rate and Kolmogorov scale above is an order-of-magnitude scaling estimate from incompressible similarity, not a measured or sourced value. The turbulence-intensity ratio of 0.25 and the dissipation formula are my assumptions.
- Wake gas temperature (hot by thousands of kelvin near the body) sets the viscosity and hence the Kolmogorov scale; I bounded it with the two columns above, but no temperature history was computed (also an open point in `laminar_wake_growth_laws.md`).
- Stratification and the background turbulence velocity of 1 to 10 metres per second at 50 to 70 km are not sourced here; Park's value of 9.1 metres per second is for his case.

## 4. Would the fragment's wake stir the droplets' vapour trail? Observations and models of wake turbulence and species mixing

### Takeaway
Droplets shed from a fragment do fall back through the fragment's own wake, at lag distances of 1 to 1000 metres within 1 millisecond to 2 seconds, but for a 10 cm fragment or a CubeSat-class parent that wake is laminar at 60 to 90 km (wake Reynolds number tens to a few hundred at ages of 0.01 to 1 second), so it carries and slowly diffuses the vapour but does not stir it turbulently; only at about 50 km, or for a metre-class parent below about 65 km, does the wake mix the vapour turbulently.

### Cited Findings
- Meteor-wake literature: the ablated vapour and plasma "experience initially strong turbulence (Lees and Hromas 1961, 1962)" during adiabatic expansion into a more stable cylinder of radius r0 in pressure but not temperature equilibrium with the air; ablated vapour mixes with entrained atmospheric gas driven toward the recompression "neck"; the same review's sketch of the near wake labels a "turbulent region" (formerly "dead water"), and says that in rarefied flow (small Reynolds number) shock waves and viscous layers are thick and a classical gas-dynamics approach is hard to apply. The review itself does not quantify the turbulence and relies on the 1961-62 Lees and Hromas papers (hypersonic blunt-body work, not meteor measurements). — [Silber et al. 2018, read in full](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07842)
- Silber et al. (2017) describe the initial trail as formed "within 10 to the minus 3 seconds" [a millisecond] by rapid adiabatic expansion accompanied by wake turbulence at temperatures up to 10,000 kelvin; they treat the first 100 milliseconds or so, and for bodies of about 4 millimetres and larger the early radial expansion can outpace ambipolar diffusion. — [Silber et al. 2017, MNRAS, read in full (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03830)
- Lin (as quoted by Lees 1964): radar echoes from the wake of Colonel Glenn's capsule at about 220,000 to 230,000 feet (67 to 70 km) show a turbulent wake of ionised gas mixed unevenly with cold air; the search-result summary adds that the mixing of ionised bow-shock gas with cold air in the turbulent wake makes the wake a strong radar scatterer. A search summary claims Lin found the brightest radar return lagged the capsule by about 0.6 seconds (4.2 kilometres), but the fetch of the paper citing it did not show this. — [Lees 1964, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356); [NASA Technical Reports Server listing, snippet only](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19690009638/downloads/19690009638.pdf); contradicted (not confirmed) by the abstract at [arXiv 2605.29124](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29124), which has no Lin citation
- Falcon 9 re-entry (2025): optically tracked meter-scale fragments, radar trail echoes from 55 to 75 km that decay with a characteristic time of about 1 second; overdense wake plasma gives echoes up to 60 decibels relative to a square metre. These are observations of the plasma wake, not of turbulent mixing. — [arXiv 2605.29124, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29124)
- Species mixing in the turbulent core: Lykoudis lists the theoretical work on the chemistry of the turbulent core (Webb and Hromas on charge exchange behind a sphere, Lin and Hayes on "inviscid random convection" versus "homogeneous mixing") and notes that the maximum density fluctuation for spheres lies near 400 diameters downstream. All these concern shock-heated air, not ablation products, and all are 1960s theory or ballistic-range work at body sizes of centimetres and pressures above 270 pascals. — [Lykoudis 1966, read in full](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493)
- Park's turbulent-wake law is used to compute dispersion of nitric oxide made by re-entering vehicles and it assumes still ambient air and turbulence caused solely by the passage of the vehicle; it was derived for a 70 km, 5.8 to 7.9 km/s lifting body of order 50 feet wake diameter. — [Park 1971, read in full](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19720024212/downloads/19720024212.pdf)
- No source found measures mixing of ablated aluminium or alumina in a fragment wake at 50 to 90 km. A modern search on re-entry aluminium oxide yielded only atmospheric-transport modelling that treats the oxide as inert and ablated mostly between 50 and 85 km. — [thesis search result, snippet only](https://utheses-gateway.univie.ac.at/api/document/get/file/81373)

### Inferences
Calculations, with the droplet drag coefficient 2.0, density 2400 kilograms per cubic metre (liquid AA7075; an assumption), fragment 10 cm with drag coefficient 1.0 and density 2810, initial speed 7 km/s, constant atmospheric density at each altitude over the short intervals shown. Droplet deceleration is 0.75 times drag coefficient times air density times speed squared over (droplet density times droplet diameter). A 200 micrometre droplet decelerates at 523 metres per second squared (53 g) at 90 km, 12,700 (1290 g) at 70 km and 157,000 (16,000 g) at 50 km, against 0.1 to 1000 times smaller values for the 10 cm fragment, so the droplet falls behind the fragment quickly. The lag times are:

| Altitude | Droplet diameter | Time to lag 1 m | 10 m | 100 m | 1 km | Droplet speed at 100 m lag (km/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 km | 200 micrometres | 3.7 ms | 12 ms | 46 ms | 0.22 s | 3.5 |
| 60 km | 200 micrometres | 6.6 ms | 22 ms | 75 ms | 0.31 s | 4.6 |
| 70 km | 200 micrometres | 12.7 ms | 41 ms | 0.14 s | 0.50 s | 5.6 |
| 80 km | 200 micrometres | 27 ms | 85 ms | 0.28 s | 0.94 s | 6.3 |
| 90 km | 200 micrometres | 62 ms | 0.20 s | 0.63 s | 2.1 s | 6.7 |
| 70 km | 20 micrometres | 4.1 ms | 14 ms | 50 ms | 0.23 s | 3.7 |

The conclusion to draw: even the biggest droplets are 100 m behind the fragment within 0.05 to 0.6 seconds, and smaller ones lag faster, so the droplets and their vapour are in the fragment's wake region for the first 1 to 10 seconds of their life. The age of the fragment wake at the droplet's position is simply the lag divided by the flight speed: about 0.14 milliseconds at a 1 metre lag, 14 milliseconds at 100 metres, 0.14 seconds at 1 kilometre (at 7 km/s).

Is that wake turbulent? Using the laminar solution for the fragment's far wake (momentum defect diffusing at the molecular rate; the same Schlichting solution as in the earlier notes), the centreline defect is U times drag-area over (8 pi times viscosity times age) and the wake Reynolds number (centreline defect times radius over viscosity) is U times drag-area over (4 pi times viscosity to the three halves times square root of age). For the 10 cm fragment at 7 km/s this gives, at ages 0.01, 0.1 and 1 second: 20,500; 6,470 and 2,050 at 50 km; 3,780; 1,200 and 378 at 60 km; 605; 191 and 60 at 70 km; 72; 23 and 7 at 80 km; 6.2; 2.0 and 0.62 at 90 km. Against the Johansson threshold of about 500 for turbulence to be sustained, the wake is laminar at 70 to 90 km at ages of 0.01 seconds and beyond, laminar at 60 km after about 0.5 seconds, and not clearly laminar at 50 km for the first second or more (consistent with the "marginal" label in section 2). The linearised far-wake solution is invalid while the defect approaches the flight speed (before about 2 milliseconds at 70 km), so the first metres behind the fragment are not covered. The laminar wake radius (1/e radius, 2 times the square root of viscosity times age) is 0.26, 0.45, 0.83, 1.7 and 3.8 metres at an age of 1 second at 50, 60, 70, 80 and 90 km; at 14 milliseconds it is about 0.03 to 0.15 metres at 70 km (add the initial radius of about 0.05 m). So a droplet released within about 0.1 metre of the axis is inside the wake gas, and a droplet released at a larger lateral offset (set by the unknown shedding velocity) is not.

What the laminar wake does to the vapour: it entrains and advects it in the flight direction at the local defect speed (hundreds of metres per second within the first 100 m, falling to 10 to 100 metres per second by 1 second at 60 to 70 km) and spreads it by molecular diffusion over 0.3 to 4 metres in a second, without any turbulent cascade. In other words, stirring by the fragment wake is at the diffusion rate that the plume model already contains. Turbulent stirring is a mechanism only for fragments of about 30 cm or larger at 50 to 60 km, or a parent of about 1 m or larger below 65 km, which is not the situation of the 10 cm fragment.

The droplet's own wake interacts with the fragment's wake only marginally: the density of the hot wake gas is lower than ambient (hot gas at ambient pressure), so the droplet's drag in the first tens of metres is somewhat smaller than assumed in the table; I did not quantify it.

### Gaps
- No observation or model of species mixing (ablation products) inside the wake of a sub-metre re-entering fragment between 50 and 90 km was found; the meteor wake literature (Silber et al., Lees and Hromas) treats initial turbulent expansion qualitatively and for millimetre to centimetre meteoroids at 80 to 110 km, where the flow is transitional. Original papers by Lees and Hromas (1961, 1962), Schapker and Camac (1969), Jenniskens et al. and ReVelle on fireball wakes were not read (blocked or not searched).
- The lateral position and velocity at which droplets are shed from the fragment (and hence whether they sit inside the wake) is unknown; I have only a bound (wake radius 0.03 to 0.3 metres at ages of 14 milliseconds).

## 5. Can the droplet's own evaporation or the rarefied expansion drive shear or instability around the vapour trail?

### Takeaway
No: for droplets of 1 to 200 micrometres the momentum left in the air, the vapour outflow and the trail's weight are all far too small to drive Kelvin-Helmholtz, Rayleigh-Taylor or buoyant instabilities. The trail only becomes unstable to ambient wind shear once it has spread to tens or hundreds of metres, which at 80 to 90 km takes minutes to an hour, i.e. in the background-turbulence regime the design already assumed.

### Cited Findings
- Unstable plasma processes do occur in meteor trails: a gradient-drift instability at trail edges grows into turbulence within about 1 to 5 milliseconds in hybrid simulations; the first three-dimensional simulations (Oppenheim and Dimant 2015) show meteor plasma trails developing instabilities, turning turbulent and diffusing inhomogeneously, with strong electric fields carving plasma channels along the geomagnetic field; and Tarnecki and Oppenheim (2021) find neutral winds drive waves and turbulence in trails with wave amplitude growing with wind speed and wavelengths tied to the trail's original density-gradient scale. These involve a plasma (ionised) trail with a steep density gradient, not the electrically neutral aluminium atoms and nanoparticles of the present problem, and I found no source discussing Kelvin-Helmholtz instability of a meteor trail. — [Oppenheim and Dimant 2015, GRL 42, 681-687 (search snippet only)](https://repositorio.igp.gob.pe/handle/IGP/3625); [Tarnecki and Oppenheim 2021 (search snippet only)](https://essopenarchive.org/doi/full/10.1002/essoar.10504729.1)
- Silber et al.'s review gives the relation between the Knudsen number of the evaporated vapour and the Reynolds number: the vapour Knudsen number is about the inverse of the Reynolds number times the ratio of the evaporated-atom speed to the sound speed, so that a low Reynolds number is equivalent to a rarefied vapour flow. — [Silber et al. 2018, read in full](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07842)

### Inferences
All numbers below are calculations, with assumptions stated.

1. Momentum left by the droplet. A droplet of drag area (drag coefficient 2 times pi over 4 times diameter squared) leaves a Gaussian momentum defect that diffuses at the molecular rate; the centreline velocity defect is speed times drag-area over (8 pi times viscosity times age), at a radius of 2 times the square root of viscosity times age. For a 200 micrometre droplet at 70 km (7 km/s), the defect is 0.10 metres per second at 1 millisecond (radius 0.026 m), 0.010 at 10 milliseconds, 0.0010 at 0.1 seconds; at 80 km (6 km/s) 0.021, 0.0021 and 0.00021; at 90 km 0.0041, 0.00041 and 0.000041. The corresponding Reynolds numbers (defect times radius over viscosity) are 0.015 or smaller at 70 km, 0.0016 or smaller at 80 km and 0.00014 or smaller at 90 km; for a 20 micrometre droplet they are another 100 times smaller. A Reynolds number of order 0.01 means the flow is dominated by viscosity, so there is nothing to shear: the droplet is a stirring source only in the sense of a sub-metre-per-second drift.

2. Vapour outflow. A droplet absorbing the free-molecular heat flux (half times density times speed cubed, with accommodation coefficient 1) can evaporate at most the heat flux divided by the latent heat of aluminium (1.08e7 joules per kilogram, from memory). That is 1.3 kilograms per square metre per second at 70 km and 7 km/s (heat flux 1.4e7 watts per square metre), 0.19 at 80 km and 6 km/s, and 0.034 at 90 km, so a 200 micrometre droplet is gone in at least 0.06 seconds at 70 km, 0.43 seconds at 80 km and 2.3 seconds at 90 km (the estimate ignores radiative loss and heating up, so true lifetimes are longer). Evaporated atoms leave at about 1500 metres per second (mean thermal speed of aluminium atoms at 2800 kelvin; 1660 at 3500 kelvin). The vapour mass deposited per metre of path is only 4e-12 kilograms per metre at 80 km (200 micrometres) and 6e-11 at 60 km, so that at 0.1 metre radius the trail density is about 3e-11 kilograms per cubic metre against air at 1.8e-5 at 80 km, a ratio of about 2e-6. The trail is a passive tracer; it cannot sink, rise or drive a Rayleigh-Taylor instability by its own weight (aluminium atoms, 27 atomic mass units, are nearly as heavy as air molecules, 29, in any case). Condensing the vapour into oxide smoke does not change this because the mass is the same.

3. Reynolds number of the trail based on its radius. For ordered flow the vapour Reynolds number is of order the inverse of its Knudsen number: with a vapour speed of 1500 metres per second and air kinematic viscosity of 0.72 square metres per second at 80 km, the radius at which the nominal Reynolds number (speed times radius over viscosity) reaches 1 is about 0.5 millimetres, and 10 centimetres gives about 200. But the speed is thermal (random), not a coherent flow, and the mean free path at 80 km is 3.8 millimetres, so any radius below a few centimetres is in the transitional regime, where "Reynolds number" has no hydrodynamic meaning. Beyond about 10 centimetres the vapour has thermalised with the ambient air and its speed falls to the diffusion speed (radius over age), so the effective Reynolds number falls to well below 1.

4. Buoyancy of a hot wake. A hot wake rises with a speed of about the square root of gravity times radius times temperature excess fraction: at most about 1.2 metres per second for the 0.15 metre wake of a 10 cm fragment at 70 km. Thermal diffusion (thermal diffusivity about viscosity over 0.71) destroys the excess in about radius squared over (4 times diffusivity), about 0.02 seconds, during which the wake rises about 0.03 metres. Buoyancy is therefore negligible for a 10 cm fragment. For a 2.8 metre parent the corresponding time is about 9 seconds and the rise tens of metres, which would matter, but not for the CubeSat-class parent.

5. Ambient wind shear on the trail. Using the standard-atmosphere temperature gradient between 70 and 90 km (minus 2.1 kelvin per kilometre at 70 to 80 km, minus 1.2 at 80 to 90 km), the buoyancy frequency is 0.018 per second at 70 to 80 km and 0.020 at 80 to 90 km (squared: 3.4e-4 and 4.1e-4 per second squared). Kelvin-Helmholtz instability requires a Richardson number (buoyancy frequency squared over shear squared) below 0.25, that is a shear above 0.037 per second at 70 to 80 km and 0.041 at 80 to 90 km (37 to 41 metres per second per kilometre). Mesospheric wind shears of that size occur in strong gravity-wave layers; I did not source a climatological value, so treat that as general knowledge, not a finding. The shear Reynolds number of the trail (shear times radius squared over viscosity, at 0.037 per second) is, with the radius grown by molecular diffusion alone (radius squared equals 4 times viscosity times age):

| Altitude | Trail radius 1 m (age; shear Reynolds number) | 10 m | 30 m | 100 m |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 70 km | 1.4 s; 0.21 | 144 s; 21 | 1300 s; 190 | 14,400 s; 2130 |
| 80 km | 0.35 s; 0.05 | 35 s; 5.2 | 314 s; 47 | 3,490 s; 517 |
| 90 km | 0.07 s; 0.01 | 6.8 s; 1.0 | 61 s; 9.1 | 682 s; 101 |

The conclusion to draw is that the trail is shear-stable (Reynolds number below 1) for radii below about 1 metre at 80 km and 10 metres at 90 km, reaches the 30 metre background inner scale given in the design after 1 to 5 minutes at 80 to 90 km, and only reaches shear Reynolds numbers of hundreds after about 10 minutes (90 km) to an hour (80 km). So the "background turbulence above 30 metres" treatment is consistent: the trail's own scale only enters the shear-driven regime when it is already about the inner scale. These ages are far shorter than the hours-long evolution; hence in the first hour the trail is stirred at its upper scales by the background atmosphere, not by anything generated by the droplet.

### Gaps
- The latent heat of aluminium, the accommodation coefficient and the droplet temperature are my assumptions (not checked in a source this session); all evaporation numbers are upper bounds on mass flux.
- No source was found on Kelvin-Helmholtz or Rayleigh-Taylor stability of a neutral vapour trail; the plasma-trail literature (Oppenheim, Dimant, Tarnecki) concerns charged-particle drift instabilities. Whether the oxidised, charged or heated vapour in the droplet trail drives such an instability was not assessed.
- The mesospheric wind shear and the Richardson number are not sourced.
- A rarefied-flow wake model for a body at Knudsen number above 10 was not found in the accessible literature (a search on direct simulation Monte Carlo found only studies of near wakes of bluff bodies, one reporting that recirculation shrinks with rarefaction in a backward-facing-step case); the Reynolds number argument for the droplet wake therefore rests on the free-molecular argument that no continuum wake structure forms, not on a published DSMC result for a sphere.
