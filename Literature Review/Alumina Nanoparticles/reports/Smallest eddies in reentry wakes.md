# Thirty metres overshoots the smallest eddies below 70 kilometres

The smallest eddies that can act on the aluminium vapour wake of a demising droplet are set by the air's viscosity, and viscosity changes far more with altitude than the turbulence does. The Kolmogorov microscale (the size of the smallest eddies before viscosity erases them) is calculated to be about 0.3 metres at 50 km, 0.5 metres at 60 km, about 1.5 to 2 metres at 70 km, about 2.5 metres at 80 km and about 6 metres at 90 km. The related inner scale of the turbulence spectrum, which is about 9.9 times larger, is about 2.6, 5, 18, 24 and 63 metres at the same altitudes. The wake column model's inner scale of 30 metres (smoothly switched on across 21 to 42 metres) is therefore **too large by roughly a factor of 10 at 50 km and 3 to 10 at 60 km, too large by about 1.5 to 3 at 70 km, about right at 80 km, and about right to slightly small at 90 km**. The more important finding is that an inner-scale switch is the wrong shape of model: the theory of how a small plume is dispersed gives a continuous, size-dependent diffusivity that grows as the plume's size to the power 4/3 and saturates at an outer scale, with no gate. At 50 to 70 km the gate withholds eddy mixing from plumes between about 1 and 21 metres wide for minutes to over an hour, which is the largest practical error. All turbulence measurements behind these numbers come from high northern latitudes (65 to 69 degrees north); nothing was found for mid-latitudes such as 40.7 degrees north, and nothing at 50 to 55 km. Body-generated wakes do not change the answer: droplets and sub-30-centimetre fragments make no turbulent wake at 60 to 90 km, so the only eddies acting on the vapour are those of the background atmosphere.

Provenance is marked throughout. "Measured" means a value taken from a published observation whose text was read. "Calculated" means arithmetic done by the note writers or by me from the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere, Sutherland's law for viscosity, and stated assumptions. "Second-hand" means a value read only through another paper's quotation, an abstract or a search summary. Where the notes disagreed, the reconciliation is given in the text.

## Altitude, not dissipation rate, sets the smallest eddy

Three quantities matter. The energy dissipation rate epsilon (the rate at which turbulence converts motion into heat, in milliwatts per kilogram, written mW/kg) sets how vigorous the turbulence is. The kinematic viscosity nu (the air's momentum diffusivity, in square metres per second) sets how large an eddy must be to survive viscous damping. The Kolmogorov microscale is eta equals the fourth root of nu cubed divided by epsilon, and the Heisenberg inner scale used by the rocket experiments is 9.9 times eta ([Lehmacher et al. 2018](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/36/1099/2018/angeo-36-1099-2018.pdf)). Measured inner scales in the mesosphere and lower thermosphere are typically 10 to 50 metres (measured, same source). Because the inner scale varies as nu to the power 3/4 divided by epsilon to the power 1/4, and nu grows by about a factor of 20 between 70 and 90 km as the air thins, altitude dominates. The table below, calculated from the standard atmosphere, shows that moving from 50 to 90 km at a fixed 10 mW/kg raises the inner scale about 60-fold, whereas changing epsilon by a factor of 100 at a fixed altitude changes it by only a factor of 3.2.

| Altitude | nu (square metres per second) | Inner scale at 1 mW/kg (m) | at 10 mW/kg (m) | at 100 mW/kg (m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 km | 0.017 | 2.6 | 1.4 | 0.8 |
| 60 km | 0.051 | 6.0 | 3.4 | 1.9 |
| 70 km | 0.174 | 15 | 8.4 | 4.7 |
| 80 km | 0.716 | 43 | 24 | 14 |
| 90 km | 3.67 | 148 | 83 | 47 |

All entries are calculated (note by the researcher on the undisturbed mesosphere; the 100 mW/kg row agrees with the 4, 10 and 40 metre values at 70, 80 and 90 km quoted by [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)). The conclusion is that 30 metres is reached by the inner scale at about 77 km for 1 mW/kg, 82 km for 10 mW/kg and 87 km for 100 mW/kg, so a single altitude-independent value can be right in only one narrow band.

Three disagreements between the notes needed reconciling. First, the notes used different viscosities at 70 km, 0.174 versus 0.3 square metres per second, which shifts the inner scale by a factor of 1.5 (the ratio to the power 3/4). The measured worked example in Lehmacher et al. 2018 sides with the larger value: a fitted inner scale of 21 metres (range 17 to 27) at 71 km with epsilon of 1.5 mW/kg (range 0.68 to 3.9) implies nu of about 0.31, not the standard-atmosphere 0.2 (measured fit, calculated inference; the cold winter profile's real density was not available). The model's own molecular diffusivity of aluminium monoxide (AlO) in air, 0.26 to 0.27 square metres per second at 70 km, is likewise 1.5 times the standard-atmosphere viscosity, and it is 1.3 times at 50 km (0.022 against 0.017) and 1.6 times at 90 km (5.9 against 3.7). That is a plausible Schmidt number below one for a heavy molecule or a different model atmosphere, but it means the 70 km inner scale is best taken as 18 metres with a range of 10 to 34, not the 15 metres that the standard atmosphere alone gives. Second, the factor of 9.9 is the Heisenberg-spectrum value that two papers read in full use ([Lehmacher et al. 2018](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/36/1099/2018/angeo-36-1099-2018.pdf); [Strelnikov et al. 2017](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/35/547/2017/)). Watkins et al. 1988 report about 13 for rocket and radar data at 80 to 87 km, with inner scales of 20 to 26 metres and epsilon of 50 to 150 mW/kg ([abstract, second-hand](https://scilit.com/publications/635bc73d8ae6ebf3fcd70fd62104c305)). One note judged that 13 does not fit with a viscosity of 2 to 4, but that viscosity belongs to 87 to 90 km; at about 80 to 83 km the viscosity is 0.7 to 1.2 and the 20 to 26 metre scale follows, so the two factors are consistent. A smaller factor for the Tatarskii spectral form is quoted from memory in one note; I could not confirm it and do not use it. The spread between 9.9 and 13 is about 30 percent, smaller than the uncertainty from viscosity or epsilon. Third, the epsilon values themselves are reconciled in the next section.

## All turbulence measurements are high-latitude and none reach 50 to 55 km

Every measured dissipation rate found was taken at Andoya (69 degrees north), Esrange (68), Poker Flat (65) or Tromso (69). **No measurement or model was found for mid-latitudes such as 40.7 degrees north, and none for 50 to 55 km**; at 60 km there is a single radar estimate that is an upper limit (about 5 mW/kg, [Latteck et al. 2005](https://meetings.copernicus.org/www.cosis.net/abstracts/EGU05/09987/EGU05-J-09987.pdf), abstract only). Every altitude-by-altitude conclusion below is therefore a high-latitude measurement carried to 40.7 degrees north on the assumption that the same physics applies, and the 50 and 60 km values are extrapolations of viscosity scaling rather than observations.

The measured values are consistent once the season, altitude and their very wide spread are taken into account. In winter at 69 degrees north the rocket mean epsilon is 1 to 2 mW/kg below 75 km and 10 to 20 mW/kg below 100 km ([Lubken et al. 1993](http://earthref.org/ERR/58965/), abstract only), rising from 1 mW/kg at 70 km to 10 at 80 and a maximum of 50 at 90 km, with the whole envelope spanning almost four decades (Szewczyk 2015, quoted second-hand in [Lehmacher et al. 2018](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/36/1099/2018/angeo-36-1099-2018.pdf)). At Poker Flat in winter 2015, layers at 71 and 75 km had medians of 1.5 and 2.7 mW/kg with an extreme of 36 mW/kg (measured, same paper). In summer the picture changes: turbulence occupies 78 to 97 km in summer but 60 to 100 km in winter, and none of seven summer flights found noticeable turbulence in the middle and lower mesosphere ([Lubken 1997](http://earthref.org/ERR/52842/), abstract only), though later campaigns found some at 70 to 82 km ([Strelnikov et al. 2017](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/35/547/2017/)). Summer values at 89 to 96 km range from about 10 to about 2000 mW/kg ([Strelnikov et al. 2009](https://www.ann-geophys.net/27/1449/2009/angeo-27-1449-2009.pdf)), while the mean over all summer flights at 80 to 90 km is about 10 mW/kg with a width of one decade ([Strelnikov et al. 2017](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/35/547/2017/)). The single-flight mean of 74 mW/kg upleg and 307 to 492 mW/kg downleg in the same campaign shows how much one profile can differ from the climatology. Radar means of 5 mW/kg at 60 km and 20 mW/kg at 80 km agree "reasonably" but are upper limits ([Latteck et al. 2005](https://meetings.copernicus.org/www.cosis.net/abstracts/EGU05/09987/EGU05-J-09987.pdf); [Hall et al. 2003](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/21/787/2003/angeo-21-787-2003.pdf)). One note quoted values near 0.4 W/kg from an early chemical-release analysis; its OCR text was garbled and the values are 10 to 40 times above the later rocket means, so I do not use them ([Justus 1965](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19660004282/downloads/19660004282.pdf)).

Two further facts qualify any single-valued epsilon. Turbulence is intermittent: at 90 to 100 km in winter small-scale isotropic turbulence was seen with only 16 percent probability, and in summer 7 of 10 layers of polar mesospheric summer echoes (radar echoes from the summer mesopause) had no neutral turbulence ([Lehmacher et al. 2018](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/36/1099/2018/angeo-36-1099-2018.pdf); [Lubken et al. 2002](https://zendy.io/title/10.1029/2001jd000915), abstract only). Layers also change within 33 minutes. So an average over a plume's life is a fraction of the in-layer value. Second, the quietest turbulence near 90 km is close to the limit where buoyancy suppresses it: using an assumed buoyancy frequency of 0.02 per second, the minimum detectable epsilon is about 1.5 mW/kg at 90 km (calculated), so there the Ozmidov scale (the largest eddy that buoyancy has not flattened) can fall below the Kolmogorov scale and no inertial range exists.

## Rees onset and the calculated inner scales agree once the concept is clarified

The one direct observation of a trail becoming turbulent is second-hand. Rees et al. 1972, as quoted by [Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf), saw chemical-release trails begin turbulent expansion 10 to 20 seconds after release at diameters of 40 to 60 metres near 88 to 93 km, giving epsilon of 10 to 20 mW/kg; the original paper was not retrieved. That is an onset radius of 20 to 30 metres, close to the model's 30 metres. The calculated inner scale at 90 km for 10 to 20 mW/kg is 70 to 83 metres, so the onset diameter is 0.5 to 0.9 times the inner scale, or roughly 4 to 7 times the Kolmogorov scale (8 to 9 metres for these values). This is not a contradiction. The inner scale is where the velocity spectrum bends away from the inertial law, whereas turbulent spreading overtakes molecular spreading from about the Kolmogorov scale upward, so onset should lie between those two. Two cautions apply: a chemical release starts as a puff already tens of metres wide, so 40 to 60 metres is an upper bound on the true onset size, and Lehmacher's own 2009 trails were judged to give dissipation estimates that were too high. Other trail evidence is at 86 to 110 km only. Persistent meteor trains at 86 to 97 km spread with eddy diffusivities of several hundred square metres per second, about 100 times molecular ([Kelley et al. 2003](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mesospheric-turbulence-measurements-from-persistent-leonid-meteor/), abstract only, with no onset radius given), and chemical trails stay turbulent to about 103 km ([Lehmacher et al. 2011](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/29/2327/2011/angeo-29-2327-2011.pdf)). Nothing comparable exists for 50 to 85 km.

## A gate is the wrong concept: the diffusivity should grow smoothly with plume size

Relative dispersion theory (how fast two nearby fluid points separate) gives three regimes ([Richardson-law literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/nlin/0107061); [direct numerical simulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1209.2296)). Below the Kolmogorov scale a plume feels only smooth strain and its effective diffusivity is of order epsilon over nu times the plume size squared. Between the Kolmogorov scale and the outer scale the diffusivity grows as epsilon to the power 1/3 times size to the power 4/3, the Richardson law, which holds only well above the dissipation scale. Beyond the outer scale it is a constant. The viscous and inertial forms both equal nu at the Kolmogorov scale (calculated by the plume-theory note, with textbook forms not re-verified), so turbulence adds little below that scale. Atmospheric and oceanic dispersion practice already uses this scale-dependent diffusivity ([Okubo 1971](https://scilit.com/publications/e2d3dee5874a1dd9a8edb6d1913af0e9), second-hand). The model's switch is therefore a step approximation of a power law, and the step is misplaced: it turns eddy diffusion on well above the scale where turbulence first matters at 50 to 70 km, and abruptly raises the diffusivity from molecular (0.27 square metres per second at 70 km) to K_eddy, where the 4/3 law at the same scales and epsilon gives 6 to 68 square metres per second at 21 to 42 metres (calculated, unit coefficient).

The table collects the recommended inner scale and the verdict. The inner scale uses epsilon centred on the measured winter means; "crossover size" is the plume size at which Richardson spreading catches up with molecular spreading (calculated by equating epsilon times time cubed with four times molecular diffusivity times time; a crude one-number estimate for plume-separation size, not radius).

| Altitude | Central epsilon, range (mW/kg) | Recommended inner scale, range (m) | Kolmogorov scale (m) | Crossover size (m) | Provenance | Is 30 m too large? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 km | 1 (0.1 to 10) | 2.6 (1.4 to 4.6) | 0.3 | 0.3 to 0.9 | calculated, epsilon unmeasured | far too large, about 10 times |
| 60 km | 2 (1 to 5; extremes 0.1 to 10) | 5 (3 to 11) | 0.5 | 0.6 to 2 | calculated; epsilon radar upper limit and rocket winter | too large, 3 to 10 times |
| 70 km | 1 (0.2 to 4) | 18 (10 to 34) | 1.8 | 2 to 8 | measured fit 21 m at 71 km; rest calculated | somewhat too large, 1.5 to 3 times |
| 80 km | 10 (1 to 100) | 24 (14 to 43) | 2.5 | 5 to 15 | calculated from measured winter mean | about right |
| 90 km | 30 (10 to 100; summer layers to 2000) | 63 (47 to 85; up to 150 for quiet turbulence) | 6 | 17 to 53 | calculated; Rees onset 20 to 30 m radius second-hand | about right to up to 2 times too small |

The verdict is that 30 metres is defensible only from about 75 km upward and the error grows steadily downward; uncertainty is a factor of 1.5 to 2 above 70 km and, because nothing measured exists, at least a factor of 3 below 60 km. The model compares a radius with the threshold while the inner scale is an eddy diameter, so the equivalent radius gate is smaller by a factor of between 1.4 and 2, which moves 80 km from "about right" toward "modestly large".

The practical consequence is large. Taking the plume to start narrow and ignoring the ramp, the time to reach a 21 metre radius where the switch begins is the radius squared divided by four times the diffusivity. At 50 km molecular diffusion alone takes about 84 minutes against 6 minutes with K_eddy added; at 60 km (molecular diffusivity interpolated at about 0.08) about 24 minutes against 1 minute; at 70 km about 7 minutes against 20 seconds; at 90 km about 19 against 7 seconds (calculated, an upper bound on the delay because a continuous K would be below K_eddy at small sizes). For an aluminium vapour plume whose chemistry depends on dilution, the gate at 50 to 70 km mostly acts as a time delay in mixing.

## Body-generated wakes are laminar or too short-lived to matter

Whether the body's own wake is turbulent depends on the freestream Reynolds number (inertial over viscous forces, based on body diameter) exceeding about 50,000 at hypersonic speed, or equivalently ambient pressure times drag length exceeding 2.0 centimetres of mercury times centimetres ([Lees 1964](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2356); [Taylor et al. 1964](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2656); [Lykoudis 1966](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3493), all read in full). Applying these (calculated, with a calibration range starting at about 270 pascals, far above the 0.2 to 80 pascals at 90 to 50 km, so boundaries are uncertain by several kilometres): droplets of 200 micrometres and 1 millimetre have Reynolds numbers of at most 96 and 482 and are in transitional or free-molecular flow, so **no continuum wake exists and none is turbulent**. A 10 centimetre fragment and a CubeSat-class parent (equal-area diameter 0.1 to 0.2 metres) are **laminar at 60 to 90 km at every speed from 2 to 8 km/s and marginal only at 50 km at 8 km/s**. A 30 centimetre body is turbulent only at about 50 km above 4 km/s. A 1 metre body is turbulent below about 64 to 69 km and a 2.8 metre spacecraft below about 72 to 77 km. Where turbulent, the wake's Kolmogorov scale is from about 1 millimetre close behind the body to centimetres and decimetres by 10 to 100 kilometres downstream (calculated by scaling, since Lykoudis states no internal turbulence measurements existed), it stays energetic for about 1 to 12 seconds and is lost in the background within about 30 seconds to 6 minutes. Those eddies are far smaller than the background inner scale, but they die long before the hours-long plume evolution. In the laminar case the fragment wake spreads the vapour by molecular diffusion at the rate the model already contains. Early meteor-trail work reached the same conclusion for ambient turbulence versus wake turbulence ([Justus 1965](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19660004282/downloads/19660004282.pdf)). The shear Reynolds number of a trail also stays below 1 until it is about 1 metre wide at 80 km and 10 metres at 90 km, so no self-generated instability appears before the plume reaches the background turbulence range (calculated; the wind shear assumed, 0.037 per second, is not sourced).

## What to change in the model, and the K_eddy mismatch

Replace the switch S(r) with a continuous capped law, a proposal from the plume-theory note that this analysis supports: D_total equals D_mol plus K(l), with K(l) equal to the smaller of K_eddy and beta times epsilon to the power 1/3 times l to the power 4/3, using l equal to about the square root of two times the radius r (the mean-square separation of a Gaussian blob is twice the per-particle variance; this mapping is the plume note's own reasoning), with beta of about 0.4 to 0.6 and the same factor of 4 in the radius update. Take epsilon from the altitude table above and cap the law where it meets K_eddy. The cap size is the Ozmidov scale, which with K_eddy tied to epsilon over the buoyancy frequency squared is the smallest size reached by the full K_eddy. This ties the inner scale to altitude automatically and removes the 21 to 42 metre gate.

A mismatch must be settled first. K_eddy is related to dissipation by K equals about 0.8 times epsilon divided by the buoyancy frequency squared (the Weinstock form; the coefficient is not verified, and [Vlasov and Kelley 2015](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/33/857/2015/) show it follows from similarity theory with an upper limit near 320 square metres per second). With the assumed buoyancy frequency of 0.02 per second, the model's K_eddy values of 0.3, 1.9, 5.5, 6.4 and 9.7 square metres per second at 50, 60, 70, 75 and 90 km imply epsilon of about 0.15, 0.9, 2.7, 3.2 and 4.8 mW/kg (my arithmetic). Against the measured high-latitude winter means this is about right at 60 km (0.9 against 1 to 2), 2 to 3 times high at 70 km (2.7 against 1), and 2 to 10 times low at 90 km (4.8 against 10 to 50). Measured upper-mesosphere diffusivities are higher still: typical 4 square metres per second in the lower and 100 in the upper mesosphere ([Lubken 1997](http://earthref.org/ERR/52842/), abstract only), and several hundred for meteor trains ([Kelley et al. 2003](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/mesospheric-turbulence-measurements-from-persistent-leonid-meteor/)), 10 to 50 times the model's 9.7 at 90 km. Two things reconcile this partly: intermittency lowers a mean value, and a one-dimensional model's K_eddy mainly parameterises large-scale circulation transport, not small-scale mixing ([1988 study, search summary, second-hand](https://earthref.org/ERR/57253)). If K_eddy is retained as a mean over occurrence, the model's value at 90 km is plausible as a mean but not as an in-layer value.

On the claim in the notes that Megner's diffusivity is no larger than molecular diffusion below about 70 km: I checked it against the model numbers and it is wrong. K_eddy exceeds the model's AlO molecular diffusivity by a factor of about 14 at 50 km and about 20 at 70 km, and the two come together only near 90 km, where K_eddy is 9.7 against 5.9 square metres per second. The note compared viscosity with a different K range; I have dropped the claim.

Decisions for you. First, whether K_eddy means an in-layer value or an occurrence-weighted mean; the first option raises K_eddy at 80 to 90 km by 2 to 10 times but spreads the plume faster than the climatological profile, the second keeps Megner's values but needs epsilon of 0.15 to 5 mW/kg in the cap law. I recommend the second, with the in-layer value as a sensitivity run. Second, how to tie epsilon to K_eddy; I recommend choosing epsilon first from the table and deriving K_eddy, since that keeps the cap scale consistent. Third, whether to use a single isotropic K; the stratified-plume simulations by [Durbeck and Gerz](https://elib.dlr.de/32304/) found late-time vertical diffusivity two orders of magnitude below horizontal for kilometre-scale aircraft plumes, which should not be transplanted to 10 to 100 metres without caution, so I recommend noting it as a limitation.

## Conclusion

The finding that changes the picture is that the question "what is the smallest eddy" is a poor guide to when turbulence starts to act on the vapour. The inner scale marks where the spectrum bends, and turbulence overtakes molecular spreading from about a tenth of that scale, so the physically relevant gate is smaller still, which strengthens the case that 30 metres is too large wherever the inner scale is below 30. At 90 km the same logic makes 30 metres a fair approximation, supported by the one trail observation (second-hand) of onset at 20 to 30 metres radius. The largest single unknown is not the inner scale but the missing data: there is no measurement at mid-latitude and none at 50 to 55 km, so the verdicts at 50 and 60 km are extrapolations, and a seasonal split (summer has little turbulence below 78 km at 69 degrees north) could change the lower-altitude picture at 40.7 degrees north.
