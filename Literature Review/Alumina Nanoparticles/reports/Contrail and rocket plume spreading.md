# A sheared Gaussian should replace the isotropic eddy

The wake column model's single isotropic eddy diffusivity should be replaced by the model that contrail science has used for thirty years: a Gaussian cross-section described by a two-by-two covariance matrix (the table of variances and the cross-covariance of position across the trail), driven by a vertical diffusivity, a horizontal diffusivity and the vertical shear of the wind (the change of horizontal wind speed with height, in metres per second per metre, which is per second). The decisive fact is that shear alone does not enlarge the cross-section area; it stretches the cross-section into a thin ribbon. The area grows only through shear and vertical diffusion together, at a rate set by the shear times the vertical diffusivity times the age squared ([Schumann 2012](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/5/543/2012/gmd-5-543-2012.pdf)). That area controls concentration, so it is the quantity the box model needs, and my calculation shows it is nearly insensitive to the horizontal diffusivity and strongly sensitive to the vertical diffusivity and the shear. Against the current model, which at 70 km gives an area of about 2.6 times 10 to the 5 square metres at 1 hour and 3.1 times 10 to the 6 square metres at 12 hours, the sheared model gives areas between 0.09 and 9 times the current value at 1 hour and between 0.4 and 108 times at 12 hours (21 to 108 times if the vertical diffusivity equals the full Megner value), depending mainly on whether the vertical diffusivity is a fifth of, equal to, or equal to the full Megner eddy diffusivity. The box model can keep one volume with A equal to two pi times the square root of the determinant of the covariance matrix, which reduces exactly to the current area when shear is zero and the diffusivities are equal. The shear inputs at 50 to 80 km are my unsourced judgement, no measurement of horizontal spreading exists at these altitudes, and the oscillating-shear coefficient is my own unverified derivation (I checked its algebra numerically, not its physics), so the replacement is a structure with ranges to be tested, not a calibrated law. This report answers all four questions asked; the numbers in section 3 and 4 are my own calculations unless a citation is given.

## Contrail models track a covariance matrix, and area grows only through shear plus vertical mixing

Contrail and aircraft-plume models treat a trail as a line source whose cross-section, in the plane normal to the trail, is Gaussian. The standard solution is by Konopka (1995) and was applied by Schumann and colleagues, by Dürbeck and Gerz, and in the Contrail Cirrus Prediction model (CoCiP, Schumann's 2012 model for climate studies). It is exact for constant diffusivities and constant shear, and it is slender, meaning it ignores diffusion along the trail axis. The concentration is the conserved mass per unit trail length divided by an effective area, multiplied by an exponential of the quadratic form in the inverse covariance matrix; the effective area is two pi times the square root of the determinant of the covariance matrix ([Schumann 2012](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/5/543/2012/gmd-5-543-2012.pdf)). The covariance evolves under three coupled equations. The horizontal variance grows by twice the horizontal diffusivity plus twice the shear times the cross-covariance; the cross-covariance grows by the shear times the vertical variance; and the vertical variance grows by twice the vertical diffusivity. CoCiP integrates these over each time step and adds a shear diffusivity, which is zero in the standard setting and unconstrained by measurement ([Schumann 2012](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/5/543/2012/gmd-5-543-2012.pdf)).

Two consequences matter for the wake column. First, with vertical diffusivity zero the area does not grow at all from shear, however strong: the ellipse becomes wider and thinner at constant area. Second, at long times the horizontal variance grows as two thirds of the shear squared times the vertical diffusivity times the age cubed, so width grows faster than linearly and the area grows as the shear times the vertical diffusivity times the age squared divided by the square root of three (I derived the last coefficient from the quoted area equation; at 12 hours, a shear of 0.01 per second and a vertical diffusivity of 1 square metre per second it gives 6.8 times 10 to the 7 square metres, matching my direct numerical integration). A single isotropic diffusivity cannot reproduce this; the minimum is two diffusivities plus the shear. An ellipse-based alternative used in a global model (Naiman and colleagues) conserves area under shear and gives nearly the same answer: after 10 hours at their first shear case the plume area was 28 times its initial value in the ellipse model against 24 times in Konopka's solution ([Naiman et al. 2010](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/10/2551/2010/acp-10-2551-2010.html)). The model becomes non-linear and time-step dependent once any diffusivity depends on plume size, as the wake column's does; CoCiP found 60-second and 3600-second steps differed by about 10 percent in dilution ([Schumann 2012](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/5/543/2012/gmd-5-543-2012.pdf)).

CoCiP closes the diffusivities with plume-scale rules. The vertical diffusivity is 0.2 times the square of a prescribed vertical turbulent velocity (0.1 metres per second) divided by the buoyancy frequency (the frequency at which a displaced air parcel oscillates in stable stratification), with the buoyancy frequency floored at 0.001 per second; the horizontal diffusivity is 0.1 times the plume depth squared times the total shear, so it grows with plume depth, and the shear diffusivity is zero ([Schumann 2012](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/5/543/2012/gmd-5-543-2012.pdf)). At cruise altitude (9.4 to 11.3 kilometres, buoyancy frequency 0.01 to 0.02 per second) these give vertical diffusivities of 0.07 to 0.2 square metres per second, matching the quoted values, and horizontal diffusivities of 1 to 10 square metres per second for 100 metre depth (my arithmetic check). Measured and large-eddy-simulation values at those altitudes are a vertical diffusivity of 0 to 0.6 and a horizontal diffusivity of 5 to 20 square metres per second for young plumes, growing to about 10 to the 4 for plumes tens of kilometres wide ([Schumann et al. 1995](https://elib.dlr.de/32042/)). The ratio of horizontal to vertical diffusivity is therefore 10 to 100 in the cruise literature, which is the opposite of the isotropic assumption. Large-eddy simulations (Dürbeck and Gerz 1996; Unterstrasser and Gierens 2010) resolve most mixing at 10 metre grid spacing but suffer numerical diffusion in shear and stratification and, in Schumann's words, cannot identify scale-dependent Lagrangian mixing, so differences between them and Gaussian models should not be used to tune diffusivities ([Unterstrasser and Gierens 2010](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/10/2017/2010/acp-10-2017-2010.html)).

Two conventions must be matched before any size is compared. The wake column's radius satisfies the radius squared equals twice the per-axis variance, so the radius is the one-over-e radius of the profile, and its area pi times the radius squared equals two pi times the variance, which is the covariance-based area with the same Gaussian. CoCiP defines the width as the square root of 8 times the horizontal variance, which is twice the wake column radius. A CoCiP width of 5 kilometres is therefore a wake column radius of 2.5 kilometres. All radii below use the wake column convention.

## Measured growth: contrails widen with shear, stratospheric rocket plumes grow linearly, and the two disagree

The contrail measurements are the cleanest. Ten plumes sampled 5 to 100 minutes after emission had nitric oxide half-widths of 500 to 2000 metres, and with constant diffusivities shear dominated lateral dispersion after about 1 hour even at a weak 0.002 per second ([Schumann et al. 1995](https://elib.dlr.de/32042/)). Ground lidar on 81 contrails 1 to 60 minutes old found horizontal growth of 18 to 140 metres per minute and vertical growth of up to 18 metres per minute, with cross-section growth of 3500 to 25000 square metres per minute (abstract-level, [Freudenthaler et al. 1995](https://earthref.org/ERR/15164/)). Satellite spreading rates for aged contrails are 2.7 to 8 kilometres per hour ([Duda et al., via NASA](https://espo.nasa.gov/glopac/node/15742)), and the empirical bulk dilution is 7000 times the age in seconds to the power 0.8, valid below about 5000 to 10000 seconds with a factor-of-3 scatter, where dilution is the ratio of plume air mass to emitted exhaust mass ([Naiman et al. 2010](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/10/2551/2010/acp-10-2551-2010.html)). Unterstrasser and Gierens report horizontal variance growing by 2 to 3 orders of magnitude while vertical variance grew only 10 to 30 percent, again showing the ribbon shape ([Unterstrasser and Gierens 2010](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/10/2017/2010/acp-10-2017-2010.html)). Most of the cited contrail numbers are abstract-level; only CoCiP, Naiman and Unterstrasser were read in full.

Stratospheric rocket plumes are different. Beiting (Aerospace Corporation, 1999) imaged nine Shuttle and Titan IV plumes at 18, 24 and 30 kilometres and found that **diameters grew linearly with time up to 50 minutes, at 4.3, 6.8 and 8.7 metres per second** respectively, with launch-to-launch scatter up to a factor of 1.6, 2.2 and 2.7, and with **no correlation with wind speed or wind shear** ([OSTI 20005912](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/20005912)). Titan IV and Shuttle plumes seen by lidar persisted up to 3 hours as layers at 18 to 43 kilometres with vertical thickness under 250 metres in over 700 profiles ([OSTI 492298](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/492298)), and aircraft saw ozone holes 4 to 8 kilometres wide within 30 minutes of launch ([Ross et al. 1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/36318)). The plumes are thin sheets, so isotropy fails in the stratosphere too. Above 100 kilometres the spreading is far larger: the 2011 final Shuttle flight (STS-135) released about 350 tonnes of water vapour at 100 to 115 kilometres, and it spread over 3000 to 4000 kilometres in 18 hours, with a fitted eddy diffusivity of 400 to 1000 square metres per second needed to carry the water down to 90 kilometres, which the authors say they do not fully understand ([Stevens et al. 2012](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/166331)). That is 40 to 500 times the Megner values, but it is a vertical-transport fit above the altitude range of interest and the horizontal spreading is dominated by large-scale winds.

I could not reconcile Beiting's lack of shear correlation with the contrail finding that shear dominates after about an hour, and the conflict should be stated as open. Four partial explanations exist. Beiting's plumes were hot, buoyant, solid-motor exhaust that was still driven by its own dynamics at ages up to 50 minutes, before the one-hour crossover where shear takes over in the contrail case. The sample is small (six Cape Canaveral cases and one Vandenberg case are described) and scatter between launches is already a factor of 1.6 to 2.7, which could hide a correlation. The wind shear in such comparisons comes from sounding or model profiles at a coarse vertical resolution, and CoCiP itself boosts model shear because coarse shear underestimates the shear a thin plume feels ([Schumann 2012](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/5/543/2012/gmd-5-543-2012.pdf)). And over one hour a growth law of width proportional to age to the power 1.5 is hard to distinguish from a straight line. One quantitative check supports the idea that shear alone is not enough there: if a plume of thickness 250 metres grows by shear alone, a diameter rate of 4.3 metres per second needs a shear of about 0.017 per second (4.3 divided by 250; my arithmetic, using the Titan lidar thickness, which was measured hours after launch and may be thinner than the plume at 50 minutes). I could not obtain Beiting's definition of diameter, the models he tested, or the shear values, so this remains unresolved. The practical lesson for the wake column is that the first-hour growth of a young plume may be set by dynamics the model does not contain, and that the shear mechanism should be tested against measurements rather than assumed.

## Carrying the model to 50 to 90 kilometres: shear inputs are unsourced, and widths depend on whether shear is sustained

What carries over is the passive-tracer Gaussian with shear, horizontal diffusivity and vertical diffusivity, and the benchmark that dilution should be compared against measured plume widths. What does not carry over is the vortex and downwash phase (the first 2 to 3 minutes, in which the aircraft wake sinks 100 to 300 metres), the initial width and depth formulas, the ice microphysics, sedimentation, and the hot-exhaust buoyancy, because a demising droplet or sub-30-centimetre fragment makes no turbulent wake at 60 to 90 kilometres (calculated in the earlier report) and the vapour begins as an almost passive line. The cruise numbers for diffusivities and buoyancy frequency also do not carry over.

I will mark the provenance of each input, because it differs. The buoyancy frequency is calculated from the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere: 0.0163, 0.0173 and 0.0220 per second at 60, 70 and 90 kilometres (buoyancy period 4.8 to 6.4 minutes); it varies by only a factor of 1.35 across the range, but it is a global-mean profile, not a 40.7 degrees north seasonal one. The shear has **no source at 50 to 80 kilometres at mid-latitude**. The measured shear data sit at 80 to 115 kilometres: chemical-release winds show large shears on the lower flank of the 100 to 110 kilometre wind maximum that are often near instability ([Larsen 2002](https://earthref.org/ERR/72620)), and sodium lidar at Fort Collins (about 40.6 degrees north, 80 to 105 kilometres, about 1600 hours) defines large shear as above 50 metres per second per kilometre, finds about 60 percent correlation of large shears with tides, and finds the mesopause most stable in summer ([Yue et al. 2010](https://ckanprod.data-commons.k8s.ucar.edu/dataset/large-wind-shears-and-stabilities-in-the-mesopause-region-observed-by-na-lidar-at-midlatitude)). My choice of a typical shear of 0.005 to 0.01 per second, with 0.03 as a strong case, is **judgement, not measurement**. The only constraint I can calculate is that shear cannot persist above about twice the buoyancy frequency, 0.035 to 0.044 per second, where the gradient Richardson number (the squared ratio of buoyancy frequency to shear) falls below one quarter and the flow becomes unstable; a shear of 0.03 per second gives a Richardson number of 0.3 at 60 kilometres and 0.5 at 90 kilometres. The vertical diffusivity has a measured support: Megner's eddy diffusivity of 1.9, 5.5 and 9.7 square metres per second at 60, 70 and 90 kilometres is the model's input, and a dissipation rate of 1 to 30 milliwatts per kilogram gives a calculated 0.5 to 15 square metres per second using a coefficient of 0.2. Lidar eddy-flux values of 43 to 300 square metres per second near the mesopause are a different quantity (transport by dissipating waves, not spreading of one tracer parcel) and sit at 85 to 100 kilometres ([Guo et al. 2017](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/first-na-lidar-measurements-of-turbulence-heat-flux-thermal-diffu/)). The horizontal diffusivity is **not measured at all** at these altitudes.

**The sources disagree on the coefficient that converts dissipation rate to diffusivity.** The earlier report used the Weinstock form, in which the diffusivity equals about 0.8 times the dissipation rate divided by the squared buoyancy frequency ([Vlasov and Kelley 2015](https://angeo.copernicus.org/articles/33/857/2015/)), while the notes behind this report used 0.2 (an assumed value, not found in a retrieved source), and Hocking (1999) notes the relation raises many issues ([Hocking 1999](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/eps1998/51/7-8/51_7-8_525/_article/-char/en)). I do not pick one. If the Megner diffusivity is fixed, the choice changes the implied dissipation rate by a factor of 4: at 70 kilometres it is 2.1 milliwatts per kilogram with 0.8 and 8.2 with 0.2 (my arithmetic, buoyancy frequency from the table above). That in turn moves the radius at which the model's Richardson term first reaches the Megner cap, from about 32 metres (coefficient 0.8) to about 23 metres (0.2) at 70 kilometres, and from 20 to 14 metres at 60 kilometres and from 38 to 27 metres at 90 kilometres. The sheared-line area depends on the vertical diffusivity, not the dissipation rate, so the choice matters for the early time and for the cap radius, not for the late-time area.

The sheared-line widths are upper bounds when shear is not sustained. I integrated the covariance equations numerically (wake column radius convention, initial radius 1.4 metres, which is assumed and irrelevant after minutes) for a shear amplitude of 0.005 and 0.01 per second and vertical diffusivities of 0.1, 1 and 5 square metres per second. With constant shear the along-shear radius is 0.4 to 5.6 kilometres at 1 hour and 16 to 232 kilometres at 12 hours. With the shear oscillating at a 12-hour tidal period the 12-hour radius falls to 3.2 to 45 kilometres, because the displacement reverses each half period and only a diffusive remainder is permanent. The oscillating-shear form is **my own derivation, not a published result**: with shear equal to its amplitude times cosine of the tidal angular frequency and uniform in height, the lasting horizontal diffusivity is the vertical diffusivity times the amplitude squared divided by twice the angular frequency squared, valid when the angular frequency is much larger than the vertical diffusivity divided by the plume's squared vertical size. I confirmed numerically that this reproduces the covariance integration (a 12-hour radius of 20.2 kilometres in both for amplitude 0.01 and vertical diffusivity 1), which verifies the algebra but not the physics, and I could not retrieve the likely reference (Young, Rhines and Garrett 1982) to check it. The result also depends on the phase: with a quarter-period offset the 1-hour area for that case is 1.3 times 10 to the 5 square metres instead of 4.5 times 10 to the 5, although the 12-hour area is identical. Gravity-wave shear (period 6 to 60 minutes) has a much smaller lasting effect, about 0.16 times the vertical diffusivity at a 6-minute period, and a shear coherent over less than the plume's vertical size is also not treated. The vertical extent stays 38 to 268 metres at 1 hour and 131 to 930 metres at 12 hours for those vertical diffusivities, so the plume is a ribbon with an aspect ratio of 25 to 250 at 12 hours.

One check against a real observation is weakly supportive. The Falcon 9 upper-stage lithium plume (reentry at about 100 kilometres) was seen by lidar 20 hours later as a layer 94.5 to 96.8 kilometres, about 2.3 kilometres thick, passing over the site in 27 to 40 minutes, which at the mean advection speed of about 22 metres per second is 35 to 50 kilometres along the wind (measured thickness; the passage-based length is my unverified arithmetic using the average speed, not the wind at the site) ([Wing et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-025-03154-8)). The current isotropic model gives a diameter of about 2.6 kilometres at 20 hours, which matches the thickness but not the length. A vertical diffusivity of 5 gives a vertical radius of 1.2 kilometres and thickness 2.4 kilometres at 20 hours (my arithmetic), matching the thickness, and a shear of 0.005 to 0.01 per second would give a length of tens of kilometres. But the source was a 20 kilometre-long trail, so an along-wind length of 35 to 50 kilometres is not independent evidence of shear, lidar smoothing was 620 metres, and this is one case at 100 kilometres.

For finite trails the geometry matters more than the diffusion. A trail 20 kilometres long descending at 5 degrees spans 1.7 kilometres of height, so a shear of 0.01 per second moves its ends apart by 17 metres per second, or 63 kilometres in an hour; a 40 metre trail moves 1.4 kilometres in an hour (calculated in the notes). Only the shear component normal to the trail changes the cross-section; the component along the trail stretches it. No retrieved paper treats a trail inclined to the horizontal or aligned with the shear. Pure stretching conserves the plume volume (it converts cross-section into length), so the box model volume is unaffected by it, which I infer from incompressibility rather than from a source.

## Area and dilution against the current model

Because shear alone leaves the area unchanged, the relevant comparison is the effective area and the resulting dilution (the inverse of the peak concentration at fixed emitted mass per metre). The baseline is the current model at 70 kilometres with the isotropic diffusivity equal to Megner's 5.5 plus a molecular 0.27, which gives a radius of 288 metres and an area of 2.6 times 10 to the 5 square metres at 1 hour, and 999 metres and 3.1 times 10 to the 6 square metres at 12 hours (calculated for a constant-diffusivity cylinder; the capped model is smaller only in the first minute or so, until the radius passes about 30 metres). In the table below, the ratio is the sheared area divided by this baseline, so a ratio of 9 means the plume is 9 times more dilute than the current model says. The horizontal diffusivity was set equal to the vertical one; setting it 10 times larger changed the area by at most 15 percent at 1 hour and by less than 1 percent at 12 hours, so the area does not depend on the horizontal diffusivity and the ratios below hold for either choice.

The conclusion to draw from the table is that the direction of the change is not fixed. The current model spreads in all directions with the full Megner diffusivity, so the sheared model dilutes a plume more than the current one only if its vertical diffusivity is close to Megner's value; if the vertical diffusivity is a fifth of Megner's (1 against 5.5), the 1-hour area is within a factor of 2 of the current model under either shear amplitude, while at 12 hours the sheared model is 4 to 22 times larger. If the vertical diffusivity is one fiftieth (0.1), the sheared model is smaller than the current one at 1 hour and equal to or smaller at 12 hours under tidal shear, which would mean the current model over-dilutes. The 1-hour area differs from the 12-hour area by different factors than in the current model, because the sheared area grows as the age squared against the current model's age to the first power, so the sheared model is relatively less dilute early and far more dilute late. Concentration at the plume centre falls by the same ratios, so at 12 hours and the central case (shear 0.01, vertical diffusivity 1, tidal) the plume is about 8 times more dilute than the current model and the constant-shear upper bound is 22 times. These are my calculations on assumed inputs; the contrail dilution law cannot validate them because it describes cruise conditions and mass ratios, not mesospheric areas.

## A concrete replacement, and what it would mean for the results

The recommended replacement is the covariance evolution with scale-dependent, capped diffusivities, written so that it reproduces the current model when shear is zero. Let the horizontal and vertical variances be sigma_yy and sigma_zz and the cross-covariance be sigma_yz (all in square metres), and let S_n(t) be the vertical shear of the wind component normal to the trail axis. Then

d sigma_yy / dt = 2 D_H + 2 S_n sigma_yz
d sigma_yz / dt = S_n sigma_zz
d sigma_zz / dt = 2 D_V

with the cross-section area A = 2 pi sqrt(sigma_yy sigma_zz - sigma_yz squared), the width radii r_y = sqrt(2 sigma_yy) and r_z = sqrt(2 sigma_zz), the vertical diffusivity D_V = D_mol + min(K_eddy, K_R(r_z)), and the horizontal diffusivity D_H = D_mol + min(f_H K_eddy, K_R(r_y)), where K_R(r) = 0.42 times the dissipation rate to the power one third times r to the power 4/3 as in the model now, D_mol is the molecular diffusivity (0.022, 0.27 and 5.9 square metres per second for aluminium monoxide at 50, 70 and 90 kilometres from the model), and the shear diffusivity is zero. With S_n zero and f_H equal to one, this is the current law because r squared equals 2 sigma gives d(r squared)/dt equal to 4 D, and both A and the area reduce to pi r squared.

For the shear I recommend S_n(t) = S_0 cos(phi) cos(2 pi t / T + theta) where S_0 is the amplitude of the total shear, phi is the angle between the shear direction and the trail-normal direction (so a trail aligned with the shear has no cross-section growth), T is 12 hours (24 hours as a variant), and theta is a random phase. The recommended central amplitude is 0.01 per second with a range of 0.005 to 0.03, capped at 2 times the buoyancy frequency (0.035 to 0.044 per second); all of this is **unsourced judgement** except the cap, which is calculated. Vertical diffusivity should be Megner's K_eddy as the upper case and one fifth of it (about 1 square metre per second at 70 kilometres, between the 0.1 and 5 of the notes) as a lower case that allows for intermittency (turbulence occupies only a fraction of the time and layers, which the earlier report found); the area scales almost linearly with it, so it is the second most important input. The horizontal-to-vertical factor f_H should be 1, with a sensitivity of 10, because cruise data show 10 to 100 but the area barely responds. The dissipation rate for K_R should be derived from K_eddy with both coefficients (0.2 and 0.8) shown, the cap radius changing by about 40 percent between them. Gravity-wave shear should be neglected in the first version.

On whether the box model can keep one volume: yes, with caveats. For a trail long compared with its width, set V = A L with A from the covariance matrix and L the trail length, using the unstretched cross-section and the original length (stretching conserves volume). For short trails (40 metres to a few kilometres) the length is smaller than the sheared width after minutes, and the current expression pi r squared L / erf(L / 2r) should be used with r equal to sqrt(A/pi) as a first approximation, replaced by a three-dimensional covariance with a shear along the axis if short-trail results matter. A second caveat comes from the Gaussian profile itself: the mass-weighted mean concentration of a Gaussian is half its peak, so a box that uses the peak concentration overestimates a second-order rate by a factor of 2 and a more strongly non-linear process such as nucleation by more (my calculation, applies equally to the current model, so it does not change the comparison). The Gaussian itself is correct only for constant shear; under oscillating shear with a limited vertical correlation scale it need not remain Gaussian, which I did not check.

The missing evidence is considerable. There are no shear statistics at 50 to 80 kilometres at mid-latitude, no measured horizontal diffusivity at any altitude below 85 kilometres, no direct measurement of the width of a mesospheric reentry trail beyond one lithium case, and no resolution of why Beiting found growth uncorrelated with shear. Useful sources not yet read include Rayleigh lidar winds to 60 to 70 kilometres, meteor-radar winds at 80 to 100 kilometres, rocket soundings, the Young, Rhines and Garrett paper on oscillating shear, the full Beiting report, and Konopka (1995).

What this means for the research is inference, not result. Because early dilution in the sheared model is no larger than in the current one for central inputs (a 1-hour area ratio of 0.9 to 1.8 for the vertical diffusivity of 1), the conclusion that particles form early in the plume, before the plume has spread much, is unlikely to change, since it depends on the first minutes in which the radius is tens of metres and shear cannot yet matter. The smoke-uptake result, which the earlier work found to be first-order, depends on dilution over hours, so it is the result most exposed: if the vertical diffusivity is near Megner's value, the 12-hour concentration is 21 to 108 times lower than the model states and smoke uptake by the vapour would be correspondingly slower; if the vertical diffusivity is a fiftieth of Megner's, the model has over-diluted and uptake would be faster.

## Decisions for you

The first decision is the vertical diffusivity, because it is the largest lever. Using Megner's K_eddy as the vertical diffusivity keeps continuity with the current model and yields 21 to 108 times lower 12-hour concentrations (tidal to constant shear); using one fifth gives a modest change at 1 hour and 4 to 22 times lower at 12 hours. I recommend running both and reporting the range, with Megner as the default so the model changes only where shear is added. The second decision is whether to adopt the tidal form or the constant-shear form; constant shear is an upper bound that is unrealistic over 12 hours, so I recommend the tidal form as default with constant shear as an upper-bound sensitivity, and recommend labelling the tidal coefficient as unverified until the reference is checked. The third is whether to settle the 0.2 versus 0.8 coefficient before using the dissipation rate; I recommend carrying both through the cap calculation, since the area does not depend on it.

## Conclusion

The finding that changes the picture is that dilution in a sheared plume is set by the product of the vertical diffusivity and the shear, not by any horizontal diffusivity, so the model's most uncertain horizontal input is the least important one, and its two best-constrained-in-principle inputs (the vertical diffusivity from Megner and a measured shear) are the ones that decide the answer. Of these, only the vertical diffusivity has any measured basis at 50 to 90 kilometres, and the shear is unmeasured there. That makes a shear measurement at 50 to 80 kilometres, not a better horizontal diffusivity, the single most valuable piece of evidence to obtain.

A second result is that the sheared model does not simply increase the dilution; whether the plume ends up more or less dilute than the current model depends on whether the vertical diffusivity is above or below about a fifth of the Megner value. The Beiting data, the only measurement of stratospheric rocket plume growth, argue against reading shear as the sole driver of growth in the first hour, so the first test of the new model should be its first-hour behaviour, not its 12-hour behaviour.
