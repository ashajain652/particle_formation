You asked me to read the Step 3 melt-spraying implementation plan, summarize its large steps at a high level, point out any questions or inconsistencies in how it is put together, and propose how to break it into smaller implementation plans that you can hand to separate agents, including which of those smaller plans could run in parallel. I read the whole document — a background sub-agent worked through it in full, section by section, since it is too long to hold in one pass — and this page is the result. All fifteen tasks, the global constraints, the measured-facts log, and the self-review notes are covered below.

What this plan actually builds

The plan implements Step 3 of the re-entry model: what happens once the surface of a re-entering sphere reaches its melting point — how the solid melts, how the resulting liquid film runs across the surface, and how droplets are sprayed off into the wake. It replaces and extends Step 2's purely thermal model. The fifteen tasks build in a fairly strict dependency order, from low-level geometry up through documentation, and fall into eight natural phases.

1. Foundations: mesh, material, and a lookup table

Task 1 builds a prism-layered surface mesh with an "active set" that tracks which mesh elements are still solid versus fully melted away, plus helper geometry (tangents, projected areas, edges) that later tasks read from. Task 2 extends the material model with latent heat, a solidus-to-liquidus melting range, and — notably — four separate enthalpy formulas (mixed-phase enthalpy, liquid-only enthalpy, mixed-phase heat capacity, and temperature-from-enthalpy) rather than one general function. Task 4 is self-contained: it solves Girin's dispersion relation (a cubic equation used later to decide when a liquid film becomes unstable) and caches the results in a lookup table. None of these three depends on anything else in this plan.

2. The thermal core rewrite

Task 3 replaces Step 2's thermal solver with one built on nodal enthalpy (heat content tracked per mesh node) instead of per-element averages, because the prototyping log shows the old element-mean approach could cycle indefinitely across the latent-heat plateau instead of converging. This is the single largest numerical redesign in the plan, and it must run on both of the project's two solver backends (skfem and FEniCSx) behind one shared interface.

3. Surface physics: flow regime, wall pressure, and the melt film

Task 5 is the largest physics module: it decides which of three flow regimes (shock-layer, merged-layer, or free-molecular) applies at each surface patch, computes wall pressure and a boundary layer, and uses the local Knudsen number to pick which melt closure applies. Task 6 builds the melt film itself — the thin liquid layer on the surface — using an unconditionally-stable implicit solver, because an earlier explicit version needed ten to a hundred thousand sub-steps per macro time-step and was judged too slow.

4. Spraying, and validation against the literature

Task 7 decides when and how droplets leave the film (thick-film, thin-film, a second regime, and a front-side Rayleigh–Taylor instability branch), and books the released mass and energy. Task 8 is a separate, parallel validation track: it reruns the model against the specific test cases published in Girin's own papers — not this project's sphere — to check the physics against literature rather than against this project's own reference runs.

5. The melting body: where everything comes together

Task 9 is the integration point. It builds the MeltingBody class that ties together feeding and freezing of mass between elements, the film's temperature and energy balance, spraying, the sequence in which elements "die" as they fully melt (with careful hand-over of their mass and heat to neighbors), and fitting a nose-cap boundary. Its code block is roughly 1,200 lines — larger than any other task's — and the plan's own text tells the reader to "read facts 25 and 26 before reading melt_step," which is the plan flagging, in its own words, that this is the densest and least obvious part of the whole document.

6. Coupling, output, and the two reference runs

Task 10 wires MeltingBody into the main simulation loop, adding melt-history columns, demise detection, VTK melt-field output, and particle files. Task 11 is different in kind from every other task: it generates and commits the two SESAM melting reference runs using the project's existing, untouched wrapper scripts — it produces data, not new code.

7. Metrics, plots, and the command line

Task 12 computes comparison metrics against the reference runs and adds seven new plots plus visual overlays (emitting patches, film-thickness frames, melting isolines). Task 13 wires seven new command-line flags and a run-naming/JSON-settings schema through the CLI; despite transcribing to roughly 500 lines, the plan describes this as conceptually simple — mostly argument parsing and dictionary wiring, not new physics.

8. Verification and documentation

Task 14 adds a reference-tier test and the verification/sensitivity driver scripts that produce the plan's headline results. Task 15 writes the README, the model-assumptions section, the spec's amendment log, and an external facts note that lives outside this repository.

What the prototyping already found

Before writing the plan, its author ran everything once in a throwaway prototype and logged 36 numbered "measured facts." A few of these matter for anyone picking up individual tasks later:

Two earlier implementations were tried and explicitly rejected: one that debited melted mass only from the element it left (rather than also crediting the element it entered) reproduced a bug where melt reappeared after it should have been gone, and one that booked the film at a mixture enthalpy (rather than the liquid-only enthalpy from Task 2) reproduced a "free melting" runaway. Both are recorded specifically so a future implementer doesn't reintroduce them while "simplifying" the bookkeeping in Task 9.

On runtime: one macro simulation time-step costs about 2.0 seconds of wall-clock time on the reference hardware. That sounds small, but a full flight that survives all the way to the ground without fully melting away can take up to about 2,357 seconds — close to 40 minutes — because it needs many more time-steps than a flight that demises partway down. The original spec's target was under 6 minutes for a full run; the measured 40-minute case directly contradicts that target, and the plan itself flags this as something that needs to be restated rather than something that has been fixed (see the inconsistencies below).

On the physics itself: with size feedback and the corrected surface-flow model, a 100 mm sphere stops demising entirely, and 29.9% of its original mass survives to reach the ground. That is a genuine new research finding, not a bug — but the plan is explicit that it is an artifact of the model's fixed body attitude (no tumbling is simulated yet), so it should be reported as a fixed-attitude result, not as a general statement about how spheres of that size behave in re-entry.

On test coverage: the project's existing unit-test tier passes 332 tests plus 1 skipped before this plan starts; this plan adds roughly 60 more unit tests, keeping that tier's runtime near 2 minutes. A second, slower "reference" tier adds the two melting runs from Task 11 and costs roughly another 2 minutes on top — a modest number by itself, but Task 14's own verification runs (the 40-minute case above) are a different order of magnitude and should be budgeted separately when you hand that task to an agent.

Questions and inconsistencies

The plan is unusually well self-documented — its 36 measured facts already function as a running risk log — but reading it end to end surfaced seven things worth deciding on or watching for before you split it up.

Attribution

The plan's commit convention names a different model than this session's

The Global Constraints section says every task's commit should end with Co-Authored-By: Claude Opus 5. Your current session is running under a different attribution convention (Claude Sonnet 5). If you hand these tasks to agents without resolving this, some commits could end up crediting a model that didn't do the work. This needs a decision before any agent commits under this plan — see the decisions section below.

Contradiction

The runtime target is stated three different ways in three places

The original spec asks for a full physics flight in under 6 minutes. Measured fact 23 shows that a flight whose remnant survives to the ground (the 29.9%-mass-remaining case above) actually takes up to about 40 minutes. Task 15's own draft amendment restates the target as "about 4 minutes on the default mesh (target 6 minutes)" — but that restatement only describes the case where the sphere fully demises partway down; it doesn't mention the much longer non-demising case the plan itself measured. Whoever writes Task 15's documentation and whoever runs Task 14's verification suite need to agree on one honest statement of the target before either is delegated, or they will silently contradict each other.

Cosmetic

The amendments list is numbered out of sequence

The spec-amendments section numbers its entries 0 through 26, and then one further entry — covering the size-feedback decision behind the 29.9%-mass finding — is labeled "13" and placed after entry 26, rather than being renumbered in. It reads as an item inserted during a later revision (dated 2026-09-21) without renumbering everything that follows it. This doesn't change the meaning of any entry, but it would confuse anyone who later cites an amendment by number.

Structure

The "self-review" section is much thinner than its heading suggests

The section titled "Self-review notes" is only two bullet points long: a table cross-referencing spec sections to tasks, and a check that public names like MeltSettings, SprayModel, and DispersionTable are used consistently across tasks. There is no separate, dedicated list of risks the author is unsure about. The real risk log is folded into the 36 measured facts instead — which is a reasonable way to write a prototyping log, but it means anyone executing a task should actually read the relevant measured facts, not assume the self-review section already surfaced everything worth worrying about.

Latent risk

Task 9's implementer needs history, not just the task text

As noted above, two specific "obvious-looking" implementations were tried during prototyping and rejected because they reproduced known bugs (melt reappearing; a free-melting runaway). Both fixes live in the measured-facts log (facts 4, 5, 25, and 26), not in Task 9's own prose. An agent working from Task 9's text alone, without that context, could plausibly re-derive and reintroduce either mistake while trying to simplify the bookkeeping.

Coverage gap

One spraying branch never fires in the real reference flights

Task 7's own text notes that its "regime 2" spraying branch is "inert on these flights and only the unit tests exercise it." That's not a defect in the plan, but it does mean that branch of code will ship having only ever been checked against synthetic test inputs, never against an actual physics run — worth knowing if that branch later needs debugging in a real flight.

Carry-forward caveat

The non-demising finding needs its caveat to travel with it

The 29.9%-mass-surviving result is a real and useful finding, but it only holds because the model does not yet simulate tumbling — the sphere is held at a fixed attitude throughout the flight. Both Task 14 (which will reproduce this number) and Task 15 (which will document it) need to state that caveat every time the number appears, so it isn't later read or cited as a general re-entry result.

Splitting this into parallel implementation plans

Since you'd rather keep the smaller plans traceable back to this document, I've kept a 1-to-1 mapping: one smaller implementation plan per existing task, numbered P1 through P15 to match Tasks 1 through 15. What changes is the order you hand them out in. Grouping them by dependency puts them into nine "waves" — plans in the same wave don't depend on each other and can go to different agents at the same time, while a plan in a later wave needs at least one plan from an earlier wave to land first.

Wave 0 — start all four immediately, in parallel

P1 — Mesh & Active SetTask 1
Depends on: Step 2's existing mesh module only. Produces: the prism-layered mesh, the active/dead element set, and the surface helpers (tangents, projected areas, edges) that P5, P6, P7, and P10 all read from.

Include the gmsh workaround as a stated constraint, not a design choice: the plan found that gmsh's boundary-layer extrusion doesn't work on the sphere primitive it uses, so prism layers are built by hand via radial projection. An agent re-deriving this from scratch might try the extrusion approach first and lose time rediscovering why it doesn't work.

P2 — Material PropertiesTask 2
Depends on: Step 2's existing material loader only. Produces: latent heat, the solidus/liquidus range, and the four enthalpy functions that P5, P7, and P10 all consume.

Decisions for you

Which model name goes in the commit trailer for these tasks?

The plan's Global Constraints specify Co-Authored-By: Claude Opus 5 on every commit; this session is currently operating under a Claude Sonnet 5 convention instead.

  • Keep the plan's original trailer. Consistent with the document as written, but only correct if Opus 5 is actually the model that will execute these tasks.
  • Switch every task to the current session's convention. Correct if Sonnet 5 (or whichever model actually runs each task) is doing the work, but requires a one-line edit to the Global Constraints section before delegating anything.
  • Decide per plan, at delegation time. Most flexible if different plans go to different models, but risks some plans being delegated before anyone remembers to set the trailer.
Recommendation: update the Global Constraints section once, now, to whichever model will actually execute the majority of these plans, so every smaller plan inherits the correct trailer automatically instead of each agent needing a separate reminder.

How to state the runtime target before P14 and P15 are delegated

The spec's original under-6-minute target is contradicted by the measured up-to-40-minute non-demising case, and Task 15's current draft restatement only covers the shorter, demising case.

  • Edit the amendments section now to state both figures honestly — roughly 4 minutes for a demising flight, up to roughly 40 minutes for a non-demising one — before splitting the plan into P14 and P15.
  • Leave it as is and let whichever agent executes P14 flag the discrepancy when they hit it. Cheaper now, but risks P14 and P15 being written independently and disagreeing with each other in the final documentation.
  • Split it into two explicit targets (typical case vs. worst case) as a permanent feature of the spec, rather than a one-off restatement.
Recommendation: the first option. It's a small, one-time edit to the master document, and it removes the chance that P14's executor and P15's executor state two different numbers in the same set of deliverables.

How tightly to scope P10 (Melting Body)

P10 is by a wide margin the largest and most tightly coupled plan — roughly 1,200 lines covering feed/freeze bookkeeping, film energy balance, spraying integration, and element-death hand-over.

  • Keep it as one plan with a mandatory review checkpoint before P11 begins, and hand the agent the two rejected-approach warnings directly in the plan text.
  • Split it further (for example, feed/freeze bookkeeping as one plan and spraying/death-cascade as another), accepting the coordination overhead of two agents sharing one class's internal state.
Recommendation: keep it as one plan. The prototyping log's own account of this task — two rejected implementations that each silently reproduced a different bug — suggests the coupling between its parts is exactly the kind of thing that gets lost at a plan boundary if it's split between two agents who don't share full context.
